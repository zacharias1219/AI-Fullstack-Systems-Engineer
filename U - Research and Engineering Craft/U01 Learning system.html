How to learn this curriculum

How do you keep 630 topics for months and say them fluently under pressure? This page covers the four mechanisms the whole plan runs on (retrieval, spacing, interleaving, spoken output), the topic package built from them, and the setup you do once: Anki, the tracker, the question log.

Study path

Before you read

Guess first. A wrong guess now makes the right answer stick later.

Group A reads a passage four times. Group B reads it once and tests itself three times. Who remembers more after 5 minutes, and who after a week?
Five minutes: the rereaders, about 83% against 71% (secondary source). One week: the self-testers, 61% against 40%. The ranking flips with delay. Rereading wins the moment you measure it, and loses when it matters.
In the kata, four retrieval passes squeezed into day 0 versus four spread over days 1, 3, 7 and 16: what retention is left at day 30 for each?
0% for the massed plan, 74% for the spaced plan (toy model, your own run). Same number of passes, very different result.
You add 43 new cards a day, not Anki's default 20. By week 12, about how many reviews a day does the kata predict, and how many minutes at 20 seconds each?
About 184 reviews a day, around 61 minutes on average and 72 at the peak. Real Anki will run higher because the kata ignores lapses.

The core idea

You do not have a learning problem. You have a forgetting problem. Understanding a topic takes an hour. Keeping it for three months takes a system, because memory decays on a curve and every unrefreshed fact slides down it. The plan is built to catch each fact on the way down, and to make you produce it from nothing, because that is what an interview asks for.

Four mechanisms do the work, in this order of importance: retrieval (answer from a blank page before you see the answer), spacing (do it again just as it starts to fade), small implementations (build the mechanism so you have something to remember with), and spoken answers (practice the output format, not only the knowledge). Interleaving, mixing topics, runs across all four so you learn to pick the right idea, not just apply the one in front of you.

Memory hook

Close the page. Set the clock. Break the toy. Say it aloud. Four verbs, four mechanisms: retrieval, spacing, the kata, the spoken answer. Interleaving is the shuffle you apply to all four on Sundays.

How it works

Retrieval beats rereading

When you reread, the text is in front of you, so everything feels familiar. Familiarity is a signal that you can recognise the answer, not that you can produce it. Retrieval forces you to rebuild the answer from cues, which strengthens that path and shows you exactly where it breaks. In Roediger and Karpicke (2006), the self-testers had read the material about 3.4 times against four for the rereaders, felt less sure, and still recalled 61% a week later against 40%. The rereaders were the more confident group.

Check: your friend rereads each page twice and says it all feels familiar. What is wrong with using that feeling as evidence?
Familiarity measures recognition, not recall. In the study the restudy group was more confident and remembered less after a week (40% against 61%). Test yourself instead.

Spacing: catch the fact on the way down

A simple model of forgetting is R = exp(-t / S), where R is the chance you can recall, t is days since the last pass and S is stability in days. Every successful retrieval raises S, and it raises it more when recall was harder, meaning R had dropped further. That is the whole reason spacing works: a pass at R near 1 teaches almost nothing, a pass at R near 0 finds nothing to strengthen. The sweet spot is in between. If you want to meet the fact at retention r, solve for t:

t = S * ln(1 / r)
r = 0.9  ->  t = 0.105 * S      (S = 10 days: review after about 1.05 days)

As S grows, the gaps grow, so the review load per card falls over time. Anki's scheduler does a version of this for you: each time you press Good, the next interval is the current interval times the card's ease, 250% by default.

Check: S = 20 days and you want to meet the fact at 90% retention. When do you review it?
t = 20 * ln(1/0.9) = 20 * 0.105, about 2.1 days. Then S grows, so the next gap is longer.

Interleaving: practice choosing

Blocked practice, ten problems of one type in a row, lets you apply the same method without deciding. Interleaved practice mixes types, so each problem makes you work out which method applies. Practice scores look worse, test scores look better. In a classroom study of grade 7 students, interleaved practice scored 72% against 38% on an unannounced test two weeks later (Rohrer, Dedrick and Burgess, 2014). Interviews are interleaved by nature: nobody announces the topic. That is why Sunday review mixes cards from every week and why station 10 of every page asks about older topics.

Check: why do interleaved learners score lower during practice yet higher on the later test?
Mixing forces them to identify the problem type each time, which is harder now but is the skill the test needs. Blocked learners never practise choosing the method.

The topic package

Every topic follows the same loop, so you never decide how to study. Read the page once. Run the kata and break it on purpose. Close the page and answer from memory. Put what you missed in the question log. Add the cards to Anki. Record a 90 second answer for T1 and T2 topics. Then retest cold at 7 and 30 days. The tracker holds the status and fills in the retest dates for you.

Cards and the question log

A card is one idea in question form with an answer under about 25 words, weighted toward why, when, what breaks and how much. A definition card tests recognition of a sentence; a why card tests whether you can reconstruct a mechanism. The question log holds every question you could not answer cleanly, with a model answer and a retest date a week out, so your misses are spaced as well. It is your highest-value deck, because it is made of your own gaps.

Check: which is the better card, "What is a KV cache?" or "Why does a KV cache shrink the cost of decoding each new token?" Say why.
The second. It asks for a mechanism you must reconstruct, not a definition you can recognise. Interviews ask why, how much and what breaks.

Kata: forgetting curve and review bill

Part 1 compares five passes over a topic under four plans using the toy forgetting model. Part 2 schedules all 3,150 cards and prints the daily review bill. The model is a toy: the constants are made up to show the shape, and you should not quote its percentages as findings.

# U01 kata: why spaced retrieval wins, and what it costs per day. Toy model, stdlib only.
# Run: python kata.py        Break it: change the constants in the BREAK block below.
import math

# ---- Part 1: forgetting curve. R = exp(-t / S), S = stability in days. TOY numbers, not measured. ----
S0 = 1.5            # stability after first exposure (days)
REREAD_GAIN = 1.1   # rereading right now barely helps: you recognise, you do not retrieve
BASE, DIFF = 0.2, 3.0   # a retrieval multiplies S by (1 + BASE + DIFF * (1 - R)): harder recall, bigger gain

def stability_after(times, day, gain):
    """Replay the plan up to `day`; return (stability, day of last pass)."""
    s, last = S0, 0.0
    for t in times:
        if t > day: break
        r = math.exp(-(t - last) / s)          # what you still remember at the moment of the pass
        s *= gain(r)
        last = t
    return s, last

retrieve = lambda r: 1 + BASE + DIFF * (1 - r)  # effortful recall: gain grows as r falls
reread = lambda r: REREAD_GAIN                  # recognition: same small gain every time

print("PART 1  toy retention (R) after learning once, then four more passes")
print(f"{'plan':34}{'day 7':>8}{'day 30':>8}{'day 90':>8}")
plans = {
    "reread 4x on day 0 (massed)": ([0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04], reread),
    "retrieve 4x on day 0 (massed)": ([0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04], retrieve),
    "retrieve day 1,3,7,16 (spaced)": ([1, 3, 7, 16], retrieve),
    "retrieve day 1,2,3,4 (too tight)": ([1, 2, 3, 4], retrieve),
}
for name, (times, gain) in plans.items():
    row = ""
    for d in (7, 30, 90):
        s, last = stability_after(times, d, gain)
        row += f"{math.exp(-(d - last) / s):8.0%}"
    print(f"{name:34}{row}")

# ---- Part 2: the daily review bill for the whole curriculum. ----
CARDS, WEEKS = 3150, 12
INTERVALS = [1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 80]   # days between reviews of one card, roughly 90% retention
SECONDS_PER_REVIEW = 20
CAP_MIN = 75                        # the project's daily card budget
# ---- BREAK block: flip these one at a time ----
SKIP_DAYS = set()                   # try {20, 21, 22}: three days with no Anki
EXTRA_CARDS_PER_DAY = 0             # try 30: writing too many cards

days = WEEKS * 7
study_days = [d for d in range(days) if d % 7 != 6]            # Mon..Sat; Sunday is review only
new_per_day = CARDS // len(study_days) + EXTRA_CARDS_PER_DAY
minutes, reviews = [], []
stage = {}                                                     # day -> list of (stage, count) due that day
for d in study_days:
    stage.setdefault(d + INTERVALS[0], []).append((0, new_per_day))
for d in range(days + 200):
    todays = stage.pop(d, [])
    if d in SKIP_DAYS:                                         # nothing done: everything slides one day
        for s, n in todays: stage.setdefault(d + 1, []).append((s, n))
        continue
    n_today = sum(n for _, n in todays)
    for s, n in todays:
        if s + 1 < len(INTERVALS):
            stage.setdefault(d + INTERVALS[s + 1], []).append((s + 1, n))
    if d < days:
        reviews.append(n_today); minutes.append(n_today * SECONDS_PER_REVIEW / 60)

print(f"\nPART 2  {CARDS} cards over {WEEKS} weeks = {new_per_day} new cards per study day, {SECONDS_PER_REVIEW}s per review")
print(f"{'week':>4}{'avg reviews/day':>17}{'peak min/day':>14}")
for w in range(WEEKS):
    chunk_r, chunk_m = reviews[w*7:(w+1)*7], minutes[w*7:(w+1)*7]
    print(f"{w+1:>4}{sum(chunk_r)/7:>17.0f}{max(chunk_m):>14.0f}")
over = sum(1 for m in minutes if m > CAP_MIN)
print(f"days over the {CAP_MIN} min cap: {over} of {days}; peak {max(minutes):.0f} min; total {sum(reviews)} reviews = {sum(minutes)/60:.1f} h")

What to notice

  1. Rows 1 and 2 use the same four passes on day 0. Retrieval beats rereading (12% against 4% at day 7), but cramming caps both: by day 30 each is at 0%.
  2. The spaced plan holds 74% at day 30 and 21% at day 90. Ignore its 100% at day 7: the last pass falls exactly on day 7. Read the day 30 and day 90 columns.
  3. The too-tight plan (day 1, 2, 3, 4) has the same four retrievals and only 22% at day 30. More passes do not help if they come while R is still near 1.
  4. New cards stay at 43 a day, yet reviews climb from 55 a day in week 1 to 184 in week 12. Reviews accumulate; this is the price of retention.
  5. The 11,696 reviews total 65.0 hours, about an eighth of the 527 hours of topic packages. The model ignores lapses and the time to first learn a card, so the real bill is higher.
Break it
  1. Skip three days. Set SKIP_DAYS = {20, 21, 22}. Predict first: do the reviews vanish, or pile up? Result: they pile up. 6 of 84 days go over the 75 minute cap and the peak reaches 143 minutes.
  2. Write too many cards. Set EXTRA_CARDS_PER_DAY = 30. Predict the number of days over the cap. Result: 36 of 84 days, and 110.3 hours in total. This is the case for the suspend rule.
  3. Remove the desirable difficulty. Set DIFF = 0.0, so a hard recall gains no more than an easy one. Predict which plan wins at day 30. Result: the spaced plan collapses to 1%. The whole spacing benefit in this toy came from that one assumption, which is a reminder that a model shows what you put in. The research results are real; the percentages here are not.
  4. Review too often. Set INTERVALS = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Result: 27 days over the cap and 92.5 hours.

Stretch. Add a lapse rate to Part 2: each review fails 10% of the time and sends the card back to stage 0. Predict whether the week 12 average goes above or below the manual's rule of thumb of about 200 a day for 20 new cards.

Interview

The question: "How do you learn new technical material and make sure you keep it?" Say this aloud against the clock. Three levels, about 90 seconds.

1 Outcome 20 sI treat learning as an engineering problem with one failure mode, which is forgetting. The goal is to answer from a blank page months later, so I build every topic around retrieval, spaced retests and a small implementation, and I track what I miss.
2 Mechanism 45 sEach topic gets one focused read. Then I run a small program of it and deliberately break it, so I see the mechanism. I close the page and answer from memory, and anything I miss goes into a question log with a model answer. Cards are one idea each, written as questions and scheduled by Anki's spaced repetition. Each topic is retested cold at 7 and 30 days. I also record a 90 second answer in three levels, outcome, mechanism and limits, because recognising an answer and saying it under pressure are different skills.
3 Internals and limits 25 sThe evidence is that self-testing gave 61% recall after a week against 40% for rereading. The cost is real: roughly 3,000 cards is dozens of hours of reviews, so I cap cards at 75 minutes a day and suspend low-value cards rather than grind. Spaced repetition keeps facts, not judgment, so I add timed mock interviews for that, and I judge the whole system by cold retest scores, not by how confident I feel.

Traps

Each one sounds right. Say what is wrong with it before you open it.

"Rereading until it feels familiar is the most reliable way to learn something."
Familiarity is recognition, not recall. In Roediger and Karpicke 2006 the rereaders were more confident and recalled 40% after a week against 61% for self-testers. Dunlosky et al. rate rereading low utility.
"Spaced repetition just means reviewing everything as often as possible."
Early reviews gain little and cost the same minutes. In the kata, passes on days 1, 2, 3, 4 keep 22% at day 30; days 1, 3, 7, 16 keep 74%. Gaps should widen as stability grows.
"Interleaving is worse than blocked practice, because you get more wrong while practising."
Lower practice scores are the point. Mixing forces you to pick the method, and the later test rewards that: 72% against 38% two weeks later in Rohrer et al. 2014.
"If I get the Anki card right, I can explain the topic in an interview."
A card tests a short fact. An interview needs a structured answer with a mechanism, a number, a failure mode and a tradeoff. That is why every T1 and T2 topic also gets a recorded 90 second answer.
