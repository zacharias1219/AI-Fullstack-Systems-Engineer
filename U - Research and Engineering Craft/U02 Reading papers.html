Reading papers

How do you filter a stack of AI papers in minutes, separate real gains from weak baselines, and verify a paper's numbers before building on it? This page covers Keshav's three-pass method, claims-versus-evidence auditing, and first-principles number checks on Vaswani et al. (2017).

Study path

Before you read

Guess first. A wrong guess now makes the right answer stick later.

In Vaswani et al. (2017), Transformer-base has vocab 37,000, d_model 512, and 6 encoder plus 6 decoder layers. If you forget that the source embedding, target embedding, and output projection share one weight matrix, how far off is your parameter count from the paper's 65M?
You overshoot by +55.3% (100.93M derived instead of 63.05M with weight tying, versus 65.0M reported). Checking parameter counts in Pass 3 catches architectural details like weight tying immediately.
Table 1 of "Attention Is All You Need" claims self-attention is faster per layer than recurrence. At what sequence length n does a Transformer-base layer (d = 512) actually stop being cheaper than an RNN layer?
At n = 512. Self-attention costs O(n^2 * d) per layer while recurrence costs O(n * d^2), so the ratio is n / d. Self-attention is cheaper per layer only when n < d (for n = 2048 and d = 512, self-attention does 4.00x more ops per layer).
Suppose you have 12 papers in your weekly reading queue and only 2 deserve a 4-hour deep read. How many hours do you waste if you skip a 10-minute Pass 1 filter and jump straight into 1-hour reads on all 12?
You burn 6.0 extra hours (20.0h total instead of 14.0h, a 43% overrun) with zero additional deep reproductions, because 8 weak papers get an hour each instead of 10 minutes.

The core idea

Reading an AI paper from page 1 to the references treats every claim as equally worth your time. Most papers in a weekly feed either solve a different problem, compare against outdated baselines, or hide key costs in an appendix. Treating paper reading as a linear book-reading task guarantees you run out of hours before you ever verify the few papers that matter.

The fix is a funnel: spend 5 to 10 minutes in Pass 1 to reject most papers, 1 hour in Pass 2 to audit figures and claims against evidence, and 4 to 5 hours in Pass 3 only on papers you plan to build on or cite as load-bearing truth. Mental model: read a paper the way a senior engineer reviews a risky RFC, starting with scope, then evidence, then math.

Memory hook

10-60-240, 5Cs then C-E-N: 10 min Pass 1 scores the 5 Cs (Category, Context, Correctness, Contributions, Clarity); 60 min Pass 2 audits Claims vs Evidence; 240 min Pass 3 verifies Numbers by virtual re-implementation.

How it works

The three-pass funnel (Keshav, 2007)

In Pass 1 (5 to 10 minutes), read only the title, abstract, introduction, section headings, conclusions, and glance at the references. Answer Keshav's five Cs: Category (measurement, system, architecture?), Context (which prior work does it build on?), Correctness (do the assumptions look valid?), Contributions (what is actually new?), and Clarity (is it written clearly?). For a queue of Q papers with Pass 1 kill rate k1 and Pass 2 kill rate k2, your total reading hours are:

H = Q * (10/60) + Q * (1 - k1) * 1.0 + Q * (1 - k1) * (1 - k2) * 4.0

Raising k1 from 0% to 67% cuts Pass 2 load by two-thirds without touching Pass 3 quality.

Claims versus evidence

In Pass 2 (about 1 hour), read the paper with care but skip dense proofs. Focus on every figure, table, and ablation. Map each headline claim in the abstract to the exact table cell that supports it. Check four failure points: weak baselines (comparing a tuned new method against an untuned 3-year-old baseline), unmatched compute (training longer or with more parameters while claiming an architectural win), missing ablations (bundling four tricks and never isolating which one moved the metric), and benchmark leakage or narrow scope (cherry-picked prompts, no variance bars, or gains smaller than seed noise). If you cannot summarize the paper's main thrust and its weakest assumption to a teammate after Pass 2, either re-read or stop.

Check: A paper claims a +2.1% accuracy gain over a strong baseline, uses 2x the training tokens, and includes no ablation isolating its new loss function. Which pass catches this, and what is your decision?
Pass 2 catches it in under an hour. Because compute is unmatched and the new loss is unablated, the evidence does not isolate the architectural claim; stop at Pass 2 and do not spend 4 hours in Pass 3.

Reproducing numbers from first principles

Pass 3 (4 to 5 hours) is virtual re-implementation: make the same assumptions as the authors, re-create the model equations and tables from scratch, and flag every discrepancy. On Vaswani et al. (2017), check two headline numbers directly from the architecture spec. First, parameter count for N=6 encoder and decoder layers, d_model=512, d_ff=2048, and shared BPE vocabulary V=37,000:

Enc layer = 4*d^2 (self-attn) + 2*d*d_ff + d_ff + d (FFN) + 4*d (2 LN) = 3.15M
Dec layer = 8*d^2 (self + cross) + 2*d*d_ff + d_ff + d (FFN) + 6*d (3 LN) = 4.20M
Total     = V*d (1 shared emb) + 6*(Enc + Dec) = 18.94M + 18.90M + 25.20M = 63.05M

Table 3 reports 65M for base (-3.0% delta) and 213M for big (214.17M derived, +0.6% delta). Landing within 5% confirms you understood weight tying and cross-attention; a 55% error tells you immediately you missed shared embeddings. Second, verify Table 1's complexity claim: self-attention costs O(n^2 * d) per layer while recurrence costs O(n * d^2), so the ratio is n / d. Self-attention is cheaper per layer only when sequence length n < d, which held for WMT translation sentences (20 to 70 tokens vs d = 512) but reverses at long contexts.

Check: Why is a 3% gap between your derived parameter count (63.05M) and a paper's table (65M) acceptable, while a 12.7% gap is a red flag?
A 1% to 5% gap usually comes from minor omitted terms (padding vocab to a multiple, extra biases, or rounding). A 12.7% gap (56.75M vs 65M) means you missed a structural block such as the 6 decoder cross-attention layers (6.29M params).

Kata: Three-pass paper audit and number verifier

Re-derives Vaswani et al. (2017) Table 3 parameter counts and Table 1 complexity crossovers from first principles, and simulates a 12-paper weekly reading queue with and without Pass 1 filtering.

kata.py

"""U02 Kata: Three-Pass Paper Audit & Number Verifier (Vaswani et al., 2017)."""

# Break-it knobs: flip these to see how paper-reading errors distort your audit
SHARE_EMBEDDINGS = True     # Break 1: set False (treats src, tgt, out as 3 matrices)
INCLUDE_CROSS_ATTN = True   # Break 2: set False (forgets decoder cross-attention)
USE_PASS1_FILTER = True     # Break 3: set False (skip 10-min Pass 1 filter on all papers)

CONFIGS = [
    ("Transformer-base", 6, 512, 2048, 8, 37000, 65.0),
    ("Transformer-big",  6, 1024, 4096, 16, 37000, 213.0),
]

def count_params_m(n_layers, d_model, d_ff, vocab, share_emb, has_cross):
    emb_copies = 1 if share_emb else 3
    emb = emb_copies * vocab * d_model
    enc_layer = 4 * d_model * d_model + 2 * d_model * d_ff + (d_ff + d_model) + 4 * d_model
    cross = 4 * d_model * d_model if has_cross else 0
    dec_layer = 4 * d_model * d_model + cross + 2 * d_model * d_ff + (d_ff + d_model) + 6 * d_model
    total = emb + n_layers * (enc_layer + dec_layer)
    return total / 1e6, emb / 1e6, (n_layers * enc_layer) / 1e6, (n_layers * dec_layer) / 1e6

print("=== PASS 3: PARAMETER COUNT VERIFICATION (Vaswani et al. 2017, Table 3) ===")
for name, n_l, d, dff, h, v, reported in CONFIGS:
    tot, emb, enc, dec = count_params_m(n_l, d, dff, v, SHARE_EMBEDDINGS, INCLUDE_CROSS_ATTN)
    err_pct = (tot - reported) / reported * 100.0
    verdict = "MATCH (<5%)" if abs(err_pct) < 5.0 else "MISMATCH (>5%)"
    print(f"{name:17s} | derived={tot:6.2f}M (emb={emb:5.2f}M enc={enc:5.2f}M dec={dec:6.2f}M) "
          f"| paper={reported:5.1f}M | delta={err_pct:+5.1f}% | {verdict}")

print("\n=== PASS 3: TABLE 1 COMPLEXITY CROSSOVER (Self-Attn O(n^2*d) vs RNN O(n*d^2)) ===")
for d in (512, 1024):
    for n in (64, 512, 2048):
        ratio = n / d  # (n^2 * d) / (n * d^2) simplifies to n / d
        winner = "Self-Attn faster" if ratio < 1 else ("Tie" if ratio == 1 else "RNN cheaper/layer")
        print(f"d={d:4d}, n={n:4d} | Attn/RNN ratio = {ratio:4.2f}x | {winner}")

print("\n=== PASS 1/2: CLAIMS-VS-EVIDENCE AUDIT & TIME BUDGET (12-paper queue) ===")
PAPER_QUEUE = [
    ("Vaswani 2017 (Transformer)", 5, True,  True,  True),
    ("Weak SOTA wrapper #1",       2, False, False, False),
    ("Weak SOTA wrapper #2",       2, True,  False, False),
    ("Solid ablation paper",       4, True,  True,  False),
    ("Marketing whitepaper",       1, False, False, False),
    ("Niche dataset paper",        3, True,  False, False),
] * 2  # 12 papers in a weekly reading queue

hours = 0.0
deep_reads = 0
for title, five_cs, has_baseline, has_ablation, has_code in PAPER_QUEUE:
    if USE_PASS1_FILTER:
        hours += 10 / 60.0  # Pass 1: 10 min
        if five_cs < 4:
            continue
    hours += 1.0  # Pass 2: 60 min (claims vs evidence check)
    evidence_score = int(has_baseline) + int(has_ablation) + int(has_code)
    if evidence_score >= 3:
        hours += 4.0  # Pass 3: 4 hr virtual re-implementation
        deep_reads += 1

print(f"Filter active: {USE_PASS1_FILTER} | Papers queued: {len(PAPER_QUEUE)} "
      f"| Pass-3 deep reads: {deep_reads} | Total hours: {hours:.1f}h")

What to notice

  1. Transformer-base derives to 63.05M vs 65.0M reported (-3.0%), and Transformer-big derives to 214.17M vs 213.0M (+0.6%). Both pass a 5% verification tolerance.
  2. In Transformer-base, the single shared embedding matrix (18.94M) is larger than all 6 encoder layers combined (18.90M).
  3. At d = 512 and n = 64 (translation sentence length), self-attention does 0.12x the per-layer ops of an RNN; at n = 2048, it flips to 4.00x more expensive per layer.
  4. On the 12-paper queue, Pass 1 filtering keeps total reading time at 14.0h while still completing both 4-hour Pass 3 deep reads.

Traps

Each one sounds right. Say what is wrong with it before you open it.

"If a paper beats the previous SOTA by 3% on a standard benchmark table, the new method is proven to be better."
Wrong. A 3% table gain can come from unmatched training compute, hyperparameter tuning only on the new method, stronger data filtering, or seed variance rather than the proposed mechanism. Without compute-matched baselines and an ablation isolating the new component, the claim is unproven.
"To really understand a paper, you should read it carefully from page 1 to the end so you do not miss any details."
Wrong. Linear reading treats weak and strong papers identically, burns ~43% more time on a typical queue (20.0h vs 14.0h on 12 papers), and traps you in dense proofs before you verify whether the baselines or assumptions are even valid.

Mixed review

Three questions from earlier topics. Nobody tells you the topic in an interview.

U01: Why does closed-book blank-page recall beat re-reading notes even when re-reading feels smoother?
Re-reading tests familiarity (recognition), which decays quickly under interview pressure. Effortful retrieval from a blank page strengthens recall pathways and exposes exact gaps immediately.
K04: What happens during prefill versus decode for a single LLM request, and which phase is memory-bandwidth bound?
Prefill processes all prompt tokens in parallel (compute-bound matmuls) to populate the KV cache. Decode generates one token per step while streaming all model weights and KV cache from HBM, making it memory-bandwidth bound.
A20: If you double context length n from 2,048 to 4,096 in standard self-attention, how much do attention FLOPs grow?
By 4x, because standard self-attention computes an n by n score matrix with O(n^2 * d) FLOPs per layer.
