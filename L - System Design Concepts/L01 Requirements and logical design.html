Requirements and logical design

What do you do in the first five minutes of a design round, before any box is drawn? You run a seven-question spine that turns a vague prompt into numbers, then into nouns and verbs. The worked problem is a reminder-call service for clinics.

Study path

Before you read

Guess first. A wrong guess now makes the right answer stick later. The problem: 200 clinics, 60 appointments each per day, one reminder call per appointment, 50% of patients answer, up to 3 attempts per patient.

How many calls does the system place per day?
About 21,000, not 12,000. Each appointment gets 1.75 calls on average: 1 + 0.5 + 0.25, because a patient who does not answer is retried. 12,000 appointments x 1.75 = 21,000. Retries are a requirement you only find by asking what happens when nobody answers.
Calls start at about 4 per second at peak and last 90 seconds. How many calls are live at once at peak?
About 394. Concurrency = arrival rate x duration (Little's law): 4.38 per second x 90 seconds. People guess 4 or 5 because they stop at the request rate. At average load it is 131, so peak is 3x average.
Is this system read-heavy or write-heavy?
Neither: about 1.1 reads per write (60,000 reads against 54,000 writes per day). Most candidates announce "read-heavy, add a cache" without estimating. Here every call attempt writes twice (start, outcome) and staff only look at a status page.

The core idea

A design prompt is a request with most of its facts missing. If you start drawing boxes, every box is a guess, and a wrong guess about scale or consistency costs more than any technology choice you make later. So you spend the first minutes asking a fixed set of questions, in a fixed order, and you write the answers down as a short contract.

The order matters because each answer constrains the next. Who and what bound the scope. How many and how well turn the scope into numbers. Nouns and verbs turn the numbers into a logical design: the entities, the operations on them, and the rules that must always hold. Limits catch what the earlier answers missed. Only after that do you choose stores, caches and queues, which is physical design.

Memory hook

A restaurant. Who is sitting at the table? What do they order, and what is not on the menu? How many tables are full at the rush? How fast must a plate arrive? The nouns are the ingredients, the verbs are the kitchen steps, and the limits are the health code. Who, What, How many, How well, Nouns, Verbs, Limits.

The index names a "seven-question spine" but does not list the seven. This page defines them as above; compare with your own lesson notes (S01.01, S01.02) and keep whichever wording you can say fluently.

How it works

Why clarify before you draw

The same prompt, "design a reminder system", can mean 20 calls a day or 2 million. A candidate who draws boxes first has silently picked one. Interviewers score how you reduce ambiguity, not how fast you reach a diagram. A requirement you did not ask about becomes an assumption, and an unstated assumption is the one that fails in production.

Questions 1 and 2: who and what

Who: name the actors, including operators and other systems. In the reminder problem they are the patient who receives the call and the clinic staff who upload schedules and read status. What: list three to five core actions for version one, each a verb, then say out loud what is out of scope. Here, rescheduling by voice and billing are out. The out-of-scope list is half the value: it bounds the design and the time.

Check: why say what is out of scope, instead of just listing what is in?
It fixes the v1 boundary so you design fewer verbs, shows you can prioritize, and gives the interviewer a chance to add one back. If they do, you add that verb and rerun questions 3 and 6 for it.

Question 3: how many

Get users, actions per user, average and peak requests per second, concurrency, data per record and growth, and the read to write ratio. Use round numbers and say them aloud. Divide per-day volume by 86,400 for an average per second, then multiply by a peak factor (2 to 5 is typical, an assumption, not a law). Concurrency comes from Little's law: concurrent = arrival rate x duration. This is only enough estimation to drive decisions; the drills are in L03.

Question 4: how well

Turn "fast, reliable, secure" into numbers: a latency percentile at a stated load, availability as a percentage with its downtime budget, durability as what may never be lost, consistency as a rule you can test, and cost per unit. "It should be fast" is not a requirement. "p99 of the status read under 300 ms at peak" is. Pick the two or three that dominate rather than listing all of them. The full treatment is L02.

Check: availability is 99.9%. How much downtime does that allow in a 30-day month, and why does this belong in the requirements?
0.1% of 43,200 minutes is 43.2 minutes. It belongs in the requirements because it decides whether you need redundancy and automatic failover, which cost money. 99.99% allows only 4.32 minutes.

Questions 5 and 6: nouns and verbs, the logical design

Logical design says what exists and what can happen, with no storage engine named. Nouns are entities with ids, ownership, relationships and states: Clinic, Patient, Appointment, Attempt. Verbs are the operations on them, each with inputs, outputs, errors and a frequency taken from question 3: upload_schedule, start_attempt, record_outcome, get_status. Add the invariants, the rules that must always hold. "Never call one patient twice for one attempt" is an invariant, and it is what later forces a unique key on (appointment_id, attempt_no). The contract details are L08.

Question 7: limits

Constraints and edges: compliance and data residency, existing stack, third-party caps, deadline and team size, and what happens when parts fail or users abuse the system. Phone numbers are personal data, so they must be findable and deletable in every store; that is the same erasure problem ForgetMeNot solves. The provider cap on concurrent calls is also a limit, and in the kata it is what breaks the design. If the domain is regulated, ask the compliance part early because it can veto stores and regions.

Check: logical vs physical design. Give one invariant from the reminder problem and say which side each part lives on.
The invariant "never call one patient twice for one attempt" is logical: it is a rule about Appointment and Attempt. Enforcing it with a unique key on (appointment_id, attempt_no) and claiming the attempt before dialing is the physical choice.

Close the loop

End by restating the contract in three or four lines and asking the interviewer to correct it. Then each number should flip a decision: 8.8 writes per second needs one relational database, 394 concurrent calls against a 300-channel cap is a blocker. If an answer changes no decision, stop asking about it. If the interviewer says "just design it", state your assumptions with numbers, mark the two that would flip the design, and go.

Kata: run the spine on a reminder-call service

The script holds the seven answers as data, derives the numbers from question 3, lints the answers for vague words, and prints the decisions the numbers force. It is the spine made executable. Then do the spoken version: set a 20-minute timer, pick a different small problem (a pastebin, a waitlist app), run the seven questions aloud using the time split in station 4, and record it.

Run

kata.py

"""spine.py: run the seven-question spine on one small problem and see what each answer forces.
Run: python kata.py            (override any number: python kata.py window_hours=6 clinics=5000)"""
import re, sys

SPEC = {
    "problem": "Clinic appointment reminder calls",
    "who":  ["patient (receives the call)", "clinic staff (upload schedule, read status)"],
    "what": ["upload schedule", "call patient 24h before", "record confirm or cancel", "show status"],
    "out":  ["reschedule by voice", "billing"],
    "scale": dict(clinics=200, appts_per_clinic_day=60, answer_rate=0.5, max_attempts=3,
                  call_seconds=90, window_hours=4, peak_factor=3,
                  staff_reads_per_clinic_hour=30, staff_hours=10, bytes_per_attempt=1000),
    "nfr": dict(status_read_p99="300 ms", availability=0.999,
                durability="never lose a confirmation",
                consistency="never call one patient twice for one attempt",
                cost="under 5 cents per call"),
    "nouns": ["Clinic", "Patient", "Appointment", "Attempt"],
    "limits": dict(channel_cap=300, notes=["phone numbers are personal data"]),
}
for arg in sys.argv[1:]:                       # key=value overrides, numbers parsed
    k, v = arg.split("=")
    v = float(v) if re.fullmatch(r"[\d.]+", v) else v
    for part in (SPEC["scale"], SPEC["nfr"], SPEC["limits"]):
        if k in part: part[k] = int(v) if isinstance(v, float) and v == int(v) else v

s, n = SPEC["scale"], SPEC["nfr"]
# --- HOW MANY: derive the numbers ---
appts = s["clinics"] * s["appts_per_clinic_day"]
per_appt = sum((1 - s["answer_rate"]) ** k for k in range(s["max_attempts"]))   # expected calls per appointment
attempts = appts * per_appt
window = s["window_hours"] * 3600
avg_rate, peak_rate = attempts / window, attempts / window * s["peak_factor"]
conc_avg, conc_peak = avg_rate * s["call_seconds"], peak_rate * s["call_seconds"]   # Little's law: L = lambda * W
writes = attempts * 2 + appts                  # start + outcome per attempt, plus the uploaded rows
reads = s["clinics"] * s["staff_reads_per_clinic_hour"] * s["staff_hours"]
peak_writes = attempts * 2 / window * s["peak_factor"]
verbs = {"upload_schedule": s["clinics"], "start_attempt": attempts, "record_outcome": attempts, "get_status": reads}

print(f"== {SPEC['problem']} ==")
print(f"appointments/day            {appts:>10,.0f}")
print(f"expected calls per appt     {per_appt:>10.2f}")
print(f"call attempts/day           {attempts:>10,.0f}")
print(f"call starts/s  avg | peak   {avg_rate:>6.2f} | {peak_rate:.2f}")
print(f"concurrent calls avg | peak {conc_avg:>6.0f} | {conc_peak:.0f}")
print(f"writes/day vs reads/day     {writes:>10,.0f} vs {reads:,.0f}  (read:write {reads / writes:.1f}:1)")
print(f"peak writes/s               {peak_writes:>10.1f}")
print(f"metadata per year (GB)      {attempts * s['bytes_per_attempt'] * 365 / 1e9:>10.1f}")
print(f"downtime budget / 30 days   {(1 - n['availability']) * 30 * 24 * 60:>10.1f} min")
print("verbs by volume/day         " + ", ".join(f"{k} {v:,.0f}" for k, v in sorted(verbs.items(), key=lambda x: -x[1])))

# --- lint: every one of the seven answers present, every NFR testable ---
print("\n== lint ==")
VAGUE = re.compile(r"\b(fast|scalable|real-?time|robust|secure|reliable|highly available)\b", re.I)
for key in ("who", "what", "out", "scale", "nfr", "nouns", "limits"):
    if not SPEC[key]: print(f"FAIL  {key}: empty")
for k, v in n.items():
    if VAGUE.search(str(v)) and not re.search(r"\d", str(v)): print(f"FAIL  nfr.{k} = '{v}': vague word, no number")
print("lint done")

# --- what the numbers force ---
print("\n== decisions forced ==")
cap = SPEC["limits"]["channel_cap"]
if conc_peak > cap: print(f"BLOCKER  peak needs {conc_peak:.0f} concurrent calls, provider cap is {cap}: widen the window, queue, or raise the cap")
else: print(f"ok       peak {conc_peak:.0f} concurrent calls fits under the cap of {cap}")
print("storage  one relational database is enough" if peak_writes < 1000 else "storage  peak writes need a closer look (partitioning or a queue)")
print("cache    reads are not dominant: skip a read cache" if reads / writes < 3 else "cache    read-heavy: a status cache pays off")
if "never" in str(n["consistency"]): print("invariant unique key on (appointment_id, attempt_no); claim the attempt before dialing")

What to notice

  1. Retries turn 12,000 appointments into 21,000 calls. The 1.75 comes from a requirement ("retry up to 3 times") and an assumption (50% answer).
  2. The call start rate is only 4.38 per second at peak, yet concurrency is 394. Duration, 90 seconds, is what makes it large.
  3. Writes per second peak at 8.8, so one relational database is plenty. The bottleneck is the telephony cap of 300, which no database choice fixes.
  4. Read:write is 1.1:1, so the read cache the "read-heavy" reflex suggests buys little.
  5. The invariant line appears only because the consistency answer contains "never". Remove the requirement and the design loses its unique key.
Break it
  1. Vague NFR. Run python kata.py status_read_p99=fast. Predict which section reports it before you run.
  2. Widen the window. Run python kata.py window_hours=6. Predict the new peak concurrency and whether the blocker survives.
  3. Scale 25x. Run python kata.py clinics=5000. Predict whether the database or the telephony cap breaks first.
What did the three break-it runs show?
  1. Lint prints FAIL for nfr.status_read_p99: vague word, no number.
  2. Peak concurrency falls to 262 (21,000 / 21,600 s x 3 x 90 s) and the blocker becomes "ok". Spreading the same calls over more hours cut peak load by a third.
  3. Attempts become 525,000 per day, peak concurrency 9,844 against a cap of 300, and peak writes only 218.8 per second. The telephony cap breaks first; the single database is still fine.

Stretch. Add a fifth verb, reschedule_by_voice, that you had put out of scope. Estimate its frequency and rerun: decide whether the new volume changes any decision.

Monitor: actual against assumed for every number from questions 3 and 4 (peak rate, concurrency, read:write, data growth). Review the design when any one drifts by a set factor, for example 2x.

Scenario: a clinic customer goes live and its 9 am burst makes half the reminder calls fail. The demo was fine. Walk through what the spine would have caught, the immediate fix, and the prevention.
  1. Diagnose: concurrency exceeded the provider channel cap. Question 3 (peak and Little's law) and question 7 (the cap) were skipped.
  2. Immediate: stagger dialing across a wider window, put a queue in front of the dialer with a rate limit equal to the cap, and ask the provider about a higher cap.
  3. Prevent: write the cap and the peak concurrency into the contract, alert at a fraction of the cap, and add admission control and backpressure (L13).
  4. Tell the customer which calls were retried and which were dropped, using the attempt records.

Interview

Question: "Before you design anything, what do you do?" Say this aloud against the clock. Three levels, about 90 seconds.

1 Outcome 20 sI turn a vague prompt into a short written contract before I draw anything: who uses it, what version one does and does not do, how big it is, and how well it must behave. That contract decides the design, so a wrong guess here costs more than any later technology choice.
2 Mechanism 45 sI run seven questions in order. Who: the actors. What: three to five core actions plus an explicit out-of-scope list. How many: users, average and peak requests per second, data growth, read to write ratio, in round numbers. How well: a latency percentile, availability, durability, consistency and cost, each with a number. Then the logical design: nouns, meaning entities, ids and relationships, and verbs, meaning operations with the frequency from question three. Last, limits: compliance, integrations, and failure behavior. Then I restate it and ask to be corrected.
3 Internals and limits 25 sNumbers earn their place by flipping decisions. Peak, not average, sets capacity, and Little's law turns a rate and a duration into concurrency. In a reminder-call example, 4.4 call starts a second at 90 seconds each means about 394 live calls, over a 300-channel provider cap, so the bottleneck is the provider, not the database. The failure mode is over-asking: past a point I state assumptions and move on.

Traps

Each one sounds right. Say what is wrong with it before you open it.

"Ask as many clarifying questions as you can. More requirements means a better design."
Unbounded questioning burns the time box and shows no judgment. Ask what would change the design, state assumptions for the rest, and stop when no remaining unknown would flip a decision.
"Design for the average load and add a buffer later."
Capacity is set by the busiest moment. In the kata the average is 131 concurrent calls and the peak is 394, a 3x gap from the peak factor; a buffer added "later" is an outage first.
"Start with the technology, because the interviewer wants to see Kafka and Redis."
The stack follows the numbers. The kata peaks at 8.8 writes per second, which one relational database handles. Infrastructure added without a number behind it is cost and failure surface (L07).
"Most systems are read-heavy, so propose a cache up front."
The read to write ratio is an estimate, not a default. The kata is 1.1:1, so a read cache buys little. Estimate it, then decide, and check how stale a cached read may be.

Question bank

Answer aloud, then open. Grade yourself honestly.

Name the seven questions of the spine in order, and say which two candidates skip most.
Who, What (with out of scope), How many, How well, Nouns, Verbs, Limits. Most often skipped: how many (no numbers, so no decisions) and out of scope (no boundary).
Follow-upWhy is the order fixed? Because each answer feeds the next: scope bounds the numbers, numbers set the NFR targets, and verbs need the frequencies from question three.
What is the difference between logical and physical design?
Logical: entities, relationships, operations and invariants, independent of any store. Physical: engines, indexes, partitioning, caches and queues, chosen to meet the numbers.
Follow-upGive an invariant that forces a physical choice. "Never call a patient twice for one attempt" forces a unique key on (appointment_id, attempt_no) and claiming the attempt before dialing.
The interviewer says "design a system for 10 million users." Turn that into numbers you can design with.
Assume 20% daily active, so 2 million. Say 5 actions each: 10 million a day. Divide by 86,400: about 116 per second average. Peak factor 3: about 350 per second. Then size a record and multiply for growth.
Follow-upWhich of those would you let the interviewer correct? The active fraction, the actions per user and the peak factor; they are assumptions, so say so.
Why is average load a trap for capacity planning?
Systems fail at the peak, and concurrency scales with rate x duration. In the kata the average is 131 live calls and the peak is 394.
Follow-upHow does Little's law apply to LLM serving? In-flight requests = arrival rate x latency. At 20 requests per second and 8 seconds each, about 160 requests are in flight, which sets how much batch and KV-cache memory you need.
Rewrite "the API should be fast and scalable" as a requirement.
Attach a percentile, a load and a number: "p99 of the status read under 300 ms at peak load of N requests per second."
Follow-upWhy a percentile, not an average? A few very slow requests vanish in a mean; p99 says one request in a hundred is slower than the number, which is what users feel.
How do you decide whether a read cache is worth proposing?
Estimate the read:write ratio, the expected hit rate and how stale a read may be. If reads do not dominate, or staleness is not allowed, skip it. The kata is 1.1:1, so skip.
What goes under Limits, and why is it last?
Compliance and residency, integrations and provider caps, existing stack, deadline, and failure and abuse behavior. It is last because it is the check that can overturn earlier choices; in a regulated domain, ask the compliance part early.
Follow-upTie it to data erasure. Every store that holds a personal field must be enumerable and deletable, so the logical design needs a data inventory per noun.
When do you stop clarifying and start designing?
When the remaining unknowns would not change the design. Time-box the spine to about 20 minutes in practice, 5 in a live 45-minute round, write the assumptions down, and move on.
The interviewer says "don't ask questions, just design it." What now?
State assumptions out loud with numbers, mark the two that would flip the design, and proceed. This keeps the contract and respects their instruction.

Mixed review

Three questions from topics that come earlier on Monday's list. Nobody tells you the topic in an interview.

D01: What does an LLM compute at each step, and why can it not produce all output tokens in parallel?
Given all tokens so far, it outputs a probability distribution over the next token. The chosen token is appended and fed back, so step t depends on step t-1. That is autoregression.
A02: You fire 50 LLM calls. Why does asyncio help, and when would it not?
The calls are network waits, so the event loop interleaves them and total time is about the slowest call, not the sum. CPU-bound work blocks the loop (and the GIL stops threads helping), so it needs processes.
K01: Where does the time go in one LLM request, end to end?
Network in, queueing, prefill (time to first token), decode (per-token time x output tokens), any tool calls or post-processing, network out.
