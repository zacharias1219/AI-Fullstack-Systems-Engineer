Back-of-the-envelope capacity planning

How do you turn product numbers like 2 million DAU and 4k-token prompts into an exact count of QPS, concurrent connections, RAM, storage, and H100 GPUs in three minutes on a whiteboard - without getting blindsided by tail-latency concurrency or the KV-cache memory wall?

Study path

Before you read

Guess first. A wrong guess now makes the right answer stick later.

A service has 2,000,000 DAU sending 5 requests per day (115.7 average QPS) with a 1.8 s mean latency. If you provision connection slots and GPU batch capacity for `115.7 * 1.8 = 208` concurrent requests, by what factor are you under-provisioned during a 3x diurnal traffic peak that coincides with a 4.2 s p99 latency spike?
You are under-provisioned by 7.0x (`1,458` slots required vs `208` provisioned). By Little's Law (`L = lambda * W`), concurrency multiplies both the arrival rate spike (`3.0x`, from `115.7` to `347.2 QPS`) and the dwell-time spike (`4.2 s / 1.8 s = 2.33x`), turning 208 average slots into 1,458 simultaneous in-flight slots.
In our kata, 347.2 peak QPS generating 160 output tokens per request requires 55,556 decode tokens/s, which fits on 18 H100 GPUs by compute throughput (3,200 tok/s per GPU). Why does expanding the context window to 16,384 tokens during a 4.2 s tail spike suddenly force you to buy 49 H100 GPUs even though total output tokens per second did not change?
Because GPU capacity is bound by `max(N_compute, N_kv_memory)`, and long-context tail spikes hit the HBM memory wall long before FLOPs saturate. An 80 GB H100 holding 16 GB of Llama-3.1-8B weights and 4 GB of runtime buffers has 60 GB left for KV cache. At 128 KB per token, a 16k-token request occupies 2.0 GB of KV cache (`30` concurrent slots per GPU). Holding `1,458` in-flight requests requires `ceil(1458 / 30) = 49` H100s - 2.7x more GPUs than compute throughput alone predicts.
Why can you not just keep a 16 GB FP16 LLM in cheap host DDR5 RAM and stream the weights over PCIe Gen5 (64 GB/s) into the GPU on each decode token instead of paying for HBM3 (3,000 GB/s)?
Because autoregressive decode is memory-bandwidth bound and must read every model weight for every generated token batch. Streaming 16 GB over HBM3 at 3,000 GB/s takes 5.33 ms per step (`187 tok/s`), whereas streaming 16 GB over PCIe Gen5 at 64 GB/s takes 250.0 ms per step (`4.0 tok/s`) - a 46.9x latency penalty that makes interactive serving impossible.

The core idea

Back-of-the-envelope capacity planning is not about guessing exact bills to three decimal places; it is about finding which physical resource binds first (network, CPU threads, RAM, disk IOPS, HBM capacity, or HBM bandwidth) within an order of magnitude before you commit to an architecture. In a system design interview or architecture review, a 3-minute calculation immediately tells you whether your data fits in a single node's RAM (`95.4 GB` FP16 vectors) or must be sharded, and whether your LLM fleet is compute-bound (`18 H100s`) or KV-cache memory-bound (`49 H100s`).

The mental model is a five-stage conversion funnel: you convert daily human behavior into arrival rate (`lambda`, in QPS), multiply by residence time (`W`, in seconds) via Little's Law to get simultaneous concurrency (`L`, in-flight slots), multiply each slot and request by its hardware footprint (bytes of KV cache, FLOPs, vector RAM, log bytes) using the latency and bandwidth hierarchy, and finally apply a headroom envelope (70% utilization ceiling plus `N+2` fault domains).

Memory hook

"QLM-SH" (Quite Large Models Demand Smart Headroom): QPS from DAU (`10^5` s/day, `3x` peak) -> Little's Law concurrency (`L = lambda_peak * W_p99`) -> Memory vs compute bound (`max(N_compute, N_kv)`) -> Stateful RAM and 3-year disk (`vectors + logs`) -> Headroom (`70%` target load + `N+2` nodes).

How it works

1. Powers of two, time shortcuts, and traffic conversion

Fast mental math relies on separating powers of 10 from powers of 2: `2^10 = 1,024 ~ 10^3` (1 KB), `2^20 ~ 10^6` (1 MB), `2^30 ~ 10^9` (1 GB), and `2^40 ~ 10^12` (1 TB). A day has `86,400` seconds, which you round to `10^5` seconds on a whiteboard (a 13.6% conservative buffer) and verify accurately when checking SLAs. A year is `3.15 * 10^7` seconds (`~pi * 10^7` s), and 3 years is roughly `10^8` seconds (`~1,095` days).

R_day      = DAU * reqs_per_user          # 2,000,000 * 5 = 10,000,000 req/day
QPS_avg    = R_day / 86,400               # 115.74 QPS (~100 QPS via 10^5 rule)
QPS_peak   = QPS_avg * peak_factor        # 115.74 * 3.0 = 347.22 QPS

Never size any synchronous tier for `QPS_avg`. Human traffic follows diurnal business curves with a `2x` to `5x` peak-to-average ratio, plus marketing or webhook bursts. In our kata, `10,000,000` daily requests equal `115.7 QPS` on average, or `347.2 QPS` at a `3x` diurnal peak.

2. Little's Law (`L = lambda * W`) and the compound tail explosion

Little's Law states that for any steady-state queueing system, the average number of items concurrently inside the system (`L`) equals the average arrival rate (`lambda`) multiplied by the average time an item spends in the system (`W`). In systems engineering, every in-flight request holds open a file descriptor, an HTTP/gRPC stream, a database connection slot, and - in LLM inference - an allocated slice of GPU HBM for its Key-Value (KV) cache from the moment prefill starts until the final `[EOS]` token leaves.

L_avg_offpeak = QPS_avg  * W_mean         # 115.74 * 1.80 s =   208 in-flight slots
L_peak_mean   = QPS_peak * W_mean         # 347.22 * 1.80 s =   625 in-flight slots
L_peak_p99    = QPS_peak * W_p99          # 347.22 * 4.20 s = 1,458 in-flight slots (7.0x!)

Notice the compound trap: when traffic surges `3x` (`347.2 QPS`) at the same time upstream tool calls or long prompts push latency from `1.8 s` to `4.2 s` (`2.33x`), concurrency does not add - it multiplies by `3.0 * 2.33 = 7.0x`, jumping from `208` slots to `1,458` simultaneous slots.

Check: If a RAG gateway receives 347.2 peak QPS and an upstream vector DB slowdown raises end-to-end request dwell time from 0.5 s to 3.5 s, what happens to the number of open worker sockets and why?
By Little's Law (`L = lambda * W`), open worker sockets jump 7x from `347.2 * 0.5 = 174` sockets to `347.2 * 3.5 = 1,215` sockets. Even though arrival QPS did not change at all, every request stays resident 7x longer, exhausting thread pools or connection limits if sized only for fast responses.

3. The latency and bandwidth hierarchy

You memorize the latency hierarchy so you can spot physically impossible designs in five seconds. Every time you cross a boundary - from SRAM to DRAM, from HBM to PCIe, from same-AZ network to cross-region WAN - latency jumps by `10x` to `1,000x` and bandwidth drops sharply:

  • CPU / GPU on-chip SRAM (L1/L2): `~1-4 ns` (`>10 TB/s`).
  • Main RAM access / GPU HBM3: `~100 ns` latency; CPU DDR5 is `~100 GB/s`, while H100 HBM3 is `~3,000 GB/s` (`3 TB/s`).
  • PCIe Gen5 x16 (CPU-to-GPU bus): `~64 GB/s` unidirectional (`46.9x` slower than HBM3).
  • NVMe SSD random read: `~16-100 us` (`~3-7 GB/s` sequential).
  • Same-datacenter / same-AZ RPC: `~0.5 ms` (`500,000 ns`).
  • Cross-continent WAN RPC (US-East to EU): `~75-150 ms`.
  • LLM prefill (2k prompt) / decode step (8B-70B FP16): `~50-200 ms` Time-To-First-Token (TTFT); `~10-30 ms` Time-Per-Output-Token (TPOT).

Our kata's Break It #2 proves why this hierarchy dictates hardware topology: streaming `16 GB` of Llama-8B FP16 weights per decode step takes `16 / 3000 = 5.33 ms` (`187 tok/s`) from HBM3, versus `16 / 64 = 250.0 ms` (`4.0 tok/s`) over PCIe Gen5.

4. LLM GPU fleet sizing: Compute floor vs KV-cache memory floor

An LLM serving fleet must satisfy two independent physical constraints simultaneously: decode token throughput and KV-cache HBM capacity. You always compute both and take the maximum:

KV_bytes_per_tok = 2 * n_layers * n_kv_heads * d_head * bytes_per_elem
# Llama-3.1-8B FP16 (GQA 8 KV heads, 32 layers, d_head=128):
# 2 * 32 * 8 * 128 * 2 = 131,072 bytes = 128 KB/token
KV_per_req_4k    = 4,096 * 128 KB = 0.50 GB/req (2.00 GB/req at 16k ctx)
Usable_KV_H100   = 80 GB - 16 GB (weights) - 4 GB (overhead) = 60.0 GB
Slots_per_GPU    = floor(60.0 GB / KV_per_req)    # 120 at 4k ctx; 30 at 16k ctx
N_gpus           = max(ceil(Peak_tok_s / GPU_tok_s), ceil(L_in_flight / Slots_per_GPU))

In our kata, `347.2 QPS * 160 output tokens = 55,556 tok/s`, which requires `ceil(55,556 / 3,200) = 18` H100 GPUs by compute. At a 4k context and `1.8 s` mean latency (`L = 625`), KV cache needs only `ceil(625 / 120) = 6` H100s, so compute binds (`18` H100s). When context expands to 16k (`30` slots/GPU) and latency hits `4.2 s` (`L = 1,458`), KV memory demands `ceil(1458 / 30) = 49` H100s!

5. Vector index RAM, multi-year storage, and `N+2` availability headroom

For retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and stateful storage, estimate both raw payload and index structural overhead:

# Vector RAM (50M passages, d = 1024):
Raw_FP16_GB      = 50M * 1024 * 2 B / 2^30         =  95.4 GB
HNSW_FP32_GB     = 50M * (1024*4 + 32*8) B / 2^30  = 202.7 GB

# 3-Year Audit Log Storage (10M req/day, 4 KB/req):
Daily_GB         = 10,000,000 * 4,096 B / 2^30     =  38.1 GB/day
Three_Year_TB    = 38.15 GB * 365 * 3 / 1024       =  40.8 TB

Finally, never run production fleets at 100% capacity. Wait times in an `M/M/1` or `M/G/k` queue grow proportional to `u / (1 - u)`, exploding once utilization `u` passes `70%-80%`. Divide active capacity by `0.7` (adding `~30-43%` headroom) and add `N+2` fault domains so one node can fail during a rolling upgrade without violating p99 SLOs.

Check: How much RAM do 50 million 1024-dimensional embeddings consume in raw FP16 versus an uncompressed FP32 HNSW index (`M=16`, roughly 32 neighbor edges x 8 bytes per vector)?
Raw FP16 takes `50,000,000 * 1,024 * 2 bytes = 95.4 GiB` (`~102.4 GB`), whereas FP32 with HNSW graph edges takes `50,000,000 * (4,096 + 256) bytes = 202.7 GiB` (`~217.6 GB`) - more than double the RAM once graph links and FP32 precision are included.

"""
L03 Kata: Back-of-the-envelope capacity planning; the latency numbers to know
Executes 10 back-of-the-envelope estimation drills (QPS, Little's Law concurrency,
GPU KV-cache capacity vs compute capacity, vector index RAM, storage growth, bandwidth,
and N+2 availability headroom) and demonstrates three sizing failures.
"""
import math

print("=== L03 KATA: 10 BACK-OF-THE-ENVELOPE CAPACITY PLANNING DRILLS ===")

# workload assumptions: 2,000,000 DAU, 5 requests/user/day, peak = 3x average
dau = 2_000_000
reqs_per_user = 5
total_daily_reqs = dau * reqs_per_user            # 10,000,000 req/day
seconds_per_day = 86_400                          # ~10^5 s/day rule of thumb
avg_qps = total_daily_reqs / seconds_per_day      # 115.74 QPS
peak_qps = avg_qps * 3.0                          # 347.22 QPS

# Latency & Little's Law (L = lambda * W)
mean_latency_s = 1.80                             # 200ms prefill + 1.6s decode (64 tokens @ 25ms)
p99_latency_s = 4.20                              # long prompt / tail decode
in_flight_avg = peak_qps * mean_latency_s         # 625 concurrent requests at peak
in_flight_p99 = peak_qps * p99_latency_s          # 1,458 slots needed during tail spike

# LLM GPU Sizing (Llama 3.1 8B FP16 on 80GB H100, S_avg = 4,096 tokens/req)
weight_gb = 16.0
overhead_gb = 4.0
usable_kv_gb = 80.0 - weight_gb - overhead_gb     # 60.0 GB for KV cache per GPU
kv_bytes_per_tok = 2 * 32 * 8 * 128 * 2           # 131,072 bytes = 128 KB/tok
kv_gb_per_req = (4096 * kv_bytes_per_tok) / (1024 ** 3)  # 0.50 GB/req at 4k context
max_batch_kv = int(usable_kv_gb // kv_gb_per_req) # 120 concurrent requests per GPU

# Throughput vs KV-memory GPU count at peak
gpu_tok_s_cap = 3200.0                            # ~3,200 decode tok/s per H100 at batch
tokens_out_per_req = 160
peak_decode_tok_s = peak_qps * tokens_out_per_req # 55,556 tok/s
gpus_by_compute = math.ceil(peak_decode_tok_s / gpu_tok_s_cap)   # 18 GPUs
gpus_by_kv_avg = math.ceil(in_flight_avg / max_batch_kv)         # 6 GPUs at 4k (or 24 at 16k!)
gpus_by_kv_16k = math.ceil(in_flight_p99 / (max_batch_kv // 4))  # 49 GPUs at 16k context + p99!

# Vector Index RAM (50M passages, d=1024, FP16 vs HNSW FP32 vs PQ8)
n_vecs, dim = 50_000_000, 1024
vec_fp32_hnsw_gb = (n_vecs * (dim * 4 + 32 * 8)) / (1024 ** 3)   # ~202.7 GB
vec_fp16_gb = (n_vecs * dim * 2) / (1024 ** 3)                   # ~95.4 GB

# Storage growth over 3 years (4 KB audit log per request)
daily_log_gb = (total_daily_reqs * 4096) / (1024 ** 3)           # 38.15 GB/day
three_year_tb = (daily_log_gb * 365 * 3) / 1024.0                # 40.80 TB

drills = [
    ("01. Daily requests (2M DAU x 5 req)",         f"{total_daily_reqs:,} req/day"),
    ("02. Average QPS (10M / 86,400s)",             f"{avg_qps:.1f} QPS (rule of 10^5: 100 QPS)"),
    ("03. Peak QPS (3x diurnal multiplier)",        f"{peak_qps:.1f} QPS"),
    ("04. Little's Law concurrency (W=1.8s)",       f"{in_flight_avg:.0f} in-flight requests"),
    ("05. Tail-spike concurrency (W_p99=4.2s)",     f"{in_flight_p99:.0f} in-flight slots"),
    ("06. Llama-8B KV cache per 4k request",        f"{kv_gb_per_req:.2f} GB ({max_batch_kv} reqs/80GB GPU)"),
    ("07. Peak decode token rate (160 tok/req)",    f"{peak_decode_tok_s:,.0f} tok/s ({gpus_by_compute} H100s by compute)"),
    ("08. GPU fleet @ 16k ctx + p99 KV ceiling",    f"{gpus_by_kv_16k} H100s (KV memory binds before FLOPs!)"),
    ("09. 50M x 1024d vectors (HNSW FP32 vs FP16)", f"{vec_fp32_hnsw_gb:.1f} GB vs {vec_fp16_gb:.1f} GB"),
    ("10. 3-year audit log storage (4 KB/req)",     f"{daily_log_gb:.1f} GB/day -> {three_year_tb:.1f} TB / 3 yrs"),
]

for title, ans in drills:
    print(f"  {title:<43} : {ans}")

What to notice

  1. The `10^5` seconds/day shortcut is within 14% of exact math: dividing `10,000,000` daily requests by `10^5` gives `100 QPS` mentally in two seconds, while exact division by `86,400 s` gives `115.7 QPS` (`347.2 QPS` at a `3x` diurnal peak).
  2. Little's Law multiplies traffic spikes by latency spikes: moving from `115.7 QPS * 1.8 s = 208` slots to `347.2 QPS * 4.2 s = 1,458` slots is a `7.0x` jump in concurrent memory and socket demand, even though traffic only rose `3.0x`.
  3. The regime flip from compute-bound (`18 H100s`) to KV-memory-bound (`49 H100s`): at 4k context (`0.50 GB` KV/req, `120` reqs/GPU), 625 concurrent requests fit on `6` GPUs so decode throughput (`55,556 tok/s` -> `18` H100s) dominates. At 16k context (`2.00 GB` KV/req, `30` reqs/GPU) and `1,458` tail slots, KV memory requires `49` H100s (`2.7x` more hardware for the exact same token throughput).
  4. HBM3 vs PCIe Gen5 is a `46.9x` cliff: reading `16 GB` of weights per decode step takes `5.33 ms` (`187 tok/s`) from HBM3 at `3,000 GB/s`, versus `250.0 ms` (`4.0 tok/s`) over PCIe Gen5 at `64 GB/s`.

Scenario: Your healthcare voice-AI assistant scales to 2M DAU (347.2 peak QPS). Product wants to attach full patient chart history, raising average prompt context from 4,096 tokens to 16,384 tokens and p99 latency to 4.2 s. Your current fleet has 24 H100 GPUs (sized as 18 compute GPUs + 33% headroom). Walk through what breaks on launch day and how you re-architect capacity.
  1. Quantify the new Little's Law concurrency: At `347.2 peak QPS` and `W_p99 = 4.2 s`, simultaneous in-flight requests reach `L = 347.2 * 4.2 = 1,458` slots.
  2. Calculate KV-cache capacity per GPU at 16k context: On Llama-3.1-8B FP16 (`128 KB/tok`), each 16k request consumes `16,384 * 128 KB = 2.00 GB` of KV cache. Each 80 GB H100 (`60 GB` usable KV) drops from `120` slots to `30` slots.
  3. Expose the outage on the 24-GPU fleet: `24 H100s * 30 slots = 720` total cluster slots - less than half of the `1,458` slots required (`49 H100s`). Once traffic crosses `50%` of peak, vLLM thrashes with KV block preemptions, latency explodes, and Little's Law locks the cluster into a tail-latency death spiral.
  4. Mitigate in software before buying hardware: Enable FP8 KV-cache quantization (`64 KB/tok`, doubling capacity from `30` to `60` slots/GPU so `1,458 / 60 = 25` H100s nearly fits) and prefix caching (since chart history is shared across multi-turn voice turns, cutting unique resident KV by `50-70%`), then add `N+2` headroom nodes.

Traps

Each one sounds right. Say what is wrong with it before you open it.

"If traffic increases 3x at peak, we just need 3x more concurrent connection and KV-cache slots than our average off-peak baseline."
Wrong because concurrency is `L = lambda * W`, and tail latency `W` also stretches under peak load. When a `3x` QPS peak (`347.2 QPS`) coincides with a `4.2 s` p99 dwell time (up from `1.8 s` mean), required slots multiply by `3.0 * (4.2 / 1.8) = 7.0x` (`1,458` slots vs `208` slots).
"Prompt prefill tokens are processed in parallel in 200 ms, so making the prompt 4x longer (4k to 16k tokens) only increases prefill FLOPs and does not change decode GPU count."
Wrong because every prompt token's Key and Value tensors must stay pinned in GPU HBM for the entire duration of autoregressive decode (`W`). Quadrupling context from 4k to 16k quadruples KV memory per request from `0.50 GB` to `2.00 GB`, cutting per-GPU batch capacity from `120` to `30` requests and raising the fleet from `18` to `49` H100s.
"50 million 1024-d float32 vectors is `50M * 1024 * 4 bytes = 200 GB`, so a single 192 GB / 200 GB RAM node can easily host our HNSW production index."
Wrong for two reasons: first, HNSW adds graph adjacency lists (`~256 bytes/vector` at `M=16`, pushing the index to `202.7 GiB` or `217.6 GB`); second, production vector nodes require `~30%` RAM headroom for OS page cache, query buffers, and background segment compaction without OOM-killing.
"If our 80 GB GPU runs out of VRAM for KV cache, we can transparently spill active KV blocks or model weights over PCIe Gen5 to host DDR5 RAM with minor latency impact."
Wrong because H100 HBM3 runs at `3,000 GB/s` while PCIe Gen5 x16 is `64 GB/s` (`46.9x` slower). Streaming 16 GB of weights or active KV pages over PCIe takes `250.0 ms` per decode step (`4.0 tok/s`) instead of `5.33 ms` (`187 tok/s`), destroying interactive SLOs.

Question bank

Answer aloud, then open. Grade yourself honestly.

How do you convert DAU into average QPS and peak QPS in your head in five seconds?
Multiply DAU by daily requests per user, divide by `10^5` seconds/day (which conservatively approximates `86,400 s/day` within 14%), and multiply by a `3x` to `5x` diurnal peak factor. For `2M DAU * 5 req/day = 10M req/day`, the `10^5` shortcut gives `100 avg QPS` (`115.7 QPS` exact) and `~300-347.2 peak QPS`.
Follow-upWhen is a 3x peak multiplier dangerously too low? When traffic is event-driven (push notifications, market open, breaking news, cron storms at the top of the hour), where peak-to-average ratios routinely reach `10x-20x`.
State Little's Law, define all three variables with units, and explain why it governs memory sizing in stateful and LLM servers.
`L = lambda * W`, where `L` is the average number of concurrent requests in flight (unitless slots), `lambda` is the arrival rate (requests/second), and `W` is the average residence time in the system (seconds). Because each in-flight request holds open a socket buffer and an LLM KV-cache reservation (`S * kv_bytes_per_tok`) for its entire lifetime `W`, peak concurrent memory equals `L * memory_per_request`.
Derive the per-token KV-cache size for Llama-3.1-8B in FP16 (`32` layers, `8` KV heads via GQA, `128` head dimension) and the total KV memory for a 4,096-token request.
Each token stores both Key and Value (`2`) across `32` layers, `8` KV heads, `128` dimensions, and `2` bytes (FP16): `2 * 32 * 8 * 128 * 2 = 131,072 bytes = 128 KB/token`. Multiplying by `4,096` tokens yields `536,870,912 bytes = 0.50 GiB` per in-flight request.
Follow-upWhat would that number be if Llama-8B used standard Multi-Head Attention (`32` KV heads) instead of GQA (`8` KV heads)? It would be `4x` larger: `512 KB/token`, or `2.00 GiB` per 4k request (`8.00 GiB` at 16k).
Write the two equations that determine the minimum GPU count for an LLM serving fleet and explain when the bottleneck flips from one to the other.
`N_compute = ceil((QPS_peak * tokens_out) / GPU_tok_s_cap)` and `N_kv = ceil((QPS_peak * W_p99) / floor(Usable_KV_GB / KV_GB_per_req))`, with `N_gpus = max(N_compute, N_kv)`. Compute binds when prompts are short and output generations are fast (`18` H100s vs `6` KV H100s at 4k context, `W=1.8 s`); KV memory binds when context length `S` or residence time `W` grows (`49` H100s vs `18` compute H100s at 16k context, `W=4.2 s`).
Why is LLM prefill compute-bound while autoregressive decode is memory-bandwidth-bound?
During prefill, all `S` prompt tokens are known at once, so matrix-matrix multiplications (`GEMM`) reuse each weight across hundreds or thousands of tokens (high arithmetic intensity, saturating Tensor Core TFLOPs). During decode, each step generates only `1` token per request in the batch, turning projections into matrix-vector-like operations (`GEMV`) that must stream all `16 GB` of weights from HBM3 (`3,000 GB/s`, `5.33 ms` floor) for only a handful of FLOPs per byte.
Follow-upHow does continuous batching improve decode throughput? By batching `B` concurrent requests together so all `B` tokens share a single `16 GB` weight read from HBM3, multiplying arithmetic intensity up to the KV-cache memory ceiling.
Give the order-of-magnitude latencies for L1/L2 SRAM, main RAM, NVMe random read, same-AZ RPC, and cross-continent WAN RPC.
L1/L2 SRAM: `~1-4 ns`; Main RAM / HBM: `~100 ns`; NVMe SSD random read: `~16-100 us`; Same-AZ datacenter RPC: `~0.5 ms` (`500 us`); Cross-continent WAN RPC: `~75-150 ms`.
How do you estimate the 3-year storage footprint for an audit log that records 4 KB per request across 10,000,000 daily requests?
Daily volume is `10,000,000 * 4 KB = 40 * 10^9 B ~ 38.1 GiB/day`. Over 3 years (`3 * 365 = 1,095 days`, or `~10^3` days), storage is `38.15 GiB * 1,095 / 1,024 = 40.8 TiB` before replication or compression.
Follow-upIf compliance requires 3x replication across availability zones, what is the raw physical disk allocation? `40.8 TiB * 3 = 122.4 TiB` raw SSD/object storage (or `~25-35 TiB` with 4x Zstd compression).
Why do we divide active capacity by 0.7 (adding ~30-43% headroom) and add N+2 nodes instead of running servers at 95% utilization?
In queueing theory (`M/G/k`), expected queue wait time scales with `u / (1 - u)` and shoots toward infinity as utilization `u` approaches `100%`; targeting `70%` load keeps queueing delay below service time during bursts. `N+2` ensures that if 1 node is offline for a rolling deployment and 1 node suffers an unexpected hardware fault, the remaining `N` nodes still carry 100% of peak traffic without cascading overload.
You have 50 million documents with 1024-d embeddings. How do you cut the in-memory vector index from 202.7 GB (HNSW FP32) to under 32 GB so it fits on a single commodity server?
Apply Product Quantization (`PQ8` or `SQ8` int8 quantization) or binary quantization with disk/FP16 rescoring: `SQ8` drops vector payload by `4x` (`200 GB -> 50 GB`), and `PQ` (`64` subspaces x `1 byte` = `64 B/vec`) compresses 50M vectors to `~3.2 GB` plus HNSW graph links (`~12 GB`), easily fitting inside `32 GB` RAM with a fast disk/RAM rerank pass.
Follow-upWhat metric must you measure when switching from FP32 HNSW to PQ8? `Recall@k` (e.g., `Recall@10` or `Recall@100`) on your domain eval set alongside end-to-end p99 retrieval latency.

Mixed review

Three questions from earlier topics. Nobody tells you the topic in an interview.

L01: During requirements clarification for an enterprise AI system, what are the five logical scoping axes you must lock down before drawing boxes or estimating capacity?
Users & traffic scale (DAU, peak QPS, read/write ratio), Latency & quality SLOs (TTFT, TPOT, p99, recall/accuracy floor), Data boundary & mutability (corpus size, freshness SLA, tenant isolation/ACL), Failure & compliance posture (auditability, PII erasure, fallback degradation), and Cost envelope (budget per 1k requests).
L02: Why is reporting mean latency instead of p95 and p99 latency dangerous in a multi-service fan-out or agentic workflow?
Because of tail-latency amplification: if one user request fans out to `k` parallel or sequential sub-calls, the probability that at least one call hits the 99th percentile grows as `1 - 0.99^k` (`~10%` of user requests hit p99 when `k=10`), and in Little's Law (`L = lambda * W`), p99 slowdowns pin concurrent memory slots and connection pools across the entire cluster.
L02: When designing a multi-region data store or RAG index, what is the real operational tradeoff described by PACELC (`if Partition: A vs C; Else: Latency vs Consistency`)?
Even when the network is healthy (`Else`), replicating writes synchronously across regions for strong consistency (`EC`) pays the `~75-150 ms` speed-of-light WAN RTT on every write, whereas asynchronous replication (`EL`) gives sub-millisecond local latency at the cost of stale reads and conflict resolution on failover.
