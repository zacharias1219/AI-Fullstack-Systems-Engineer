Engineer voice vs business voice

How do you say what you built so a technical interviewer hears mechanism, numbers, failure modes and tradeoffs, and not a pitch? This page teaches the pattern and drills it on five rewrites.

Study path

Before you read

Guess first. A wrong guess now makes the right answer stick later.

The kata scores five weak answers and five strong ones on four markers: a number, a mechanism, a failure mode, a tradeoff. How many of the four markers do the five weak answers hit, in total, out of 20?
Zero. Every weak answer scored 0 on all four markers, and every strong answer scored 4 of 4. Weak answers read fine aloud, which is the danger: nothing in them is wrong, and nothing in them can be checked.
The strong answers in the kata run 40 to 54 words, the weak ones 12 to 18. At a speaking pace of about 150 words per minute, how long does a strong first answer take to say?
About 16 to 22 seconds. The strong answer is roughly three times longer than the weak one, and still short. Engineer voice is dense, not long.
"We cut inference cost by 40%." It has a number. Why does an interviewer still hear business voice?
The number has no baseline, no unit of work and no mechanism. The interviewer cannot tell 40% of what, measured how, or caused by what. A number only helps when the listener can step into it with the next question and you have the next sentence ready.

The core idea

Business voice reports what happened: costs fell, quality rose, the system scales. It is true and unusable, because nothing in it can be checked. Engineer voice explains why it happened, so the listener can size it, reproduce it or break it. This is the gap from your 13 Nov interview. Your answers named the outcome, and the interviewer wanted the machinery behind it.

Every claim about a system can carry four parts. The mechanism is what the system does, per request or per token, that makes the claim true. The number is a measured quantity with a unit and a baseline. The failure mode is the condition under which the claim stops being true. The tradeoff is what you paid, on some axis: latency, memory, accuracy, money, complexity. Then, in one sentence, you say why the business cares.

Mental model: treat every claim you make as a function call that the interviewer can step into with a debugger. If the call has a body (mechanism), arguments you can print (numbers), a known exception path (failure) and a cost (tradeoff), they can step in all day. If it is a stub that returns "efficient", they step in once and find nothing.

Memory hook

Five questions, in order: how does it work, how much, how does it break, what does it cost, so what. A claim that cannot answer the first four is a slogan. The fifth is the only part a businessman needs, and it goes last.

How it works

The mechanism: the "because"

The mechanism is the sentence that follows "because". "Prefix caching saves money" is a claim. "The provider stores the computed key and value state for a prompt prefix, and bills cached tokens at a lower rate, but only when the next request starts with the identical tokens" is a mechanism. Notice it names what the system stores, what it reuses and the condition for reuse. Aim for one mechanism you can explain in two sentences over five tools you can only name.

The number: unit, baseline, tail

A useful number has three things. A unit of work (per request, per token, per call), a baseline (compared with what) and, for anything about time, a tail. Averages hide the slow requests. If the mean is 400 ms and 1 request in 20 takes 3 seconds, the caller who hits it hangs up. Quote p95, and p99 when timeouts matter. A share also works as a number: "3,000 of 4,200 input tokens were the system prompt" tells the listener where the lever is.

Check: "latency dropped from 2 s to 1 s". Name two things missing before an engineer would trust it.
The statistic (mean, p50, p95) and the conditions: load, prompt length, hardware, model. A halving of the mean can coexist with an unchanged p95. Also missing is the cause, the mechanism, which is what lets the listener judge whether it will hold in production.

The failure mode: when it stops being true

Every technique has a condition under which it stops working. Naming it is the strongest signal of experience, because only someone who ran the thing has seen it break. Common shapes: a miss (a cache or retrieval that does not hit), staleness (data older than the truth), overflow (a context or memory limit), drift (inputs change and quality slides silently), and bursts (load spikes past capacity). For each claim, ask "what input makes this false?" and say it.

The tradeoff: the price, on a named axis

Nothing is free; the question is which axis pays. Streaming tokens into speech cuts the time until the caller hears something, and costs you clipped sentences. A second model call that verifies answers cuts hallucinations, and adds a call of latency and money. Always name the axis: "it costs latency", not "there are tradeoffs". "There are tradeoffs" is itself business voice.

Check: "We use RAG because it is flexible and cost-effective." Name the tradeoff axis that is missing and the failure mode that is missing.
Axis: RAG adds a retrieval hop to every request, so it costs latency (50 to 150 ms in the kata example, illustrative) and another system to run. Failure: it fails when the right chunk is not in the top k results retrieved. Both are what a technical listener waits to hear.

The rewrite procedure

  1. Circle the fluff verbs. Optimized, streamlined, leveraged, robust, seamless, scalable. Each one hides a change you must name.
  2. Ask what happens per request. Walk one request or one token through the system. The step you changed is the mechanism.
  3. Find the measured quantity. Unit, baseline, tail. If you have none, say it is an estimate, or say how you would measure it.
  4. Ask when it breaks. Pick the input that makes the claim false. That is the failure mode.
  5. Name the price. Latency, memory, accuracy, money or complexity. Pick one axis and say it.

Then add the business sentence last: "That cut input spend on the long prompts, which is where most of the bill sat." One sentence, never a replacement for the four parts. You want to be credible as both CTO and CEO, so the business sentence is a skill too, in its place at the end.

Check: rewrite "We made our voice agent more responsive" using the five steps. Say what you would need to know before you could.
You need the per-hop timing: speech to text, model, text to speech. Then: "I timed each hop. The model's first token was the largest slice, so I stream tokens into speech instead of waiting for the full reply. That cuts time to first audio. The cost is clipped sentences, so I flush at sentence ends. I track p95." Numbers here come from your own measurement, so you cannot fill them in without it.

Kata: a smoke detector for engineer voice

This is the "rewrite five answers" kata, made runnable. It holds five weak answers (business voice) and five strong rewrites, and scores each on the four markers by keyword. It is a smoke alarm, not a grader: it shows you what the markers look like in text, and the break-it steps show exactly where it lies. All numbers inside the answers are illustrative, not your results.

Run

kata.py

"""V01 kata: a smoke detector for engineer voice. Crude on purpose."""
import re

MARKS = {  # the four things a strong answer contains
    "number":    r"\d",
    "mechanism": r"\b(because|so i|so that|which means|instead of|first|then|per token|prefix|stream(?:s|ed|ing)?|batch\w*|cite|checks?|queue|index\w*)\b",
    "failure":   r"\b(breaks?|fails?|misses?|miss|stale|drift|overflow|glitch\w*|clipped|unsupported|bottleneck|burst\w*)\b",
    "tradeoff":  r"\b(the cost is|the price|but|instead|trade\w*|adds?|higher|extra|not for)\b",
}
FLUFF = r"\b(streamlined?|optimi[sz]ed?|robust|seamless|leverage|efficient|significantly|reliable|trustworthy|scales?|cost-effective|flexible|guardrails)\b"

PAIRS = [
 ("Optimize tokens",
  "We streamlined our prompts and used caching to make the system more efficient and cut costs significantly.",
  "I log tokens per call by part. The system prompt was 3,000 of 4,200 input tokens, so I put it first and turned on prefix caching, which bills cached tokens at a fraction of the price. The cost is a miss whenever anything dynamic sits before the prefix, so I watch cache hit rate."),
 ("Voice agent speed",
  "We optimized the pipeline end to end so callers get a seamless, real-time experience.",
  "I timed each hop. The model's first token was 600 ms of a 1.4 s turn, so I stream tokens into speech instead of waiting for the full reply. The cost is clipped sentences, so I flush at sentence ends. I track p95, not the average."),
 ("Hallucinations",
  "We put robust guardrails in place and keep a human in the loop to ensure reliable, trustworthy output.",
  "Every claim must cite a retrieved passage, and a second check rejects claims with no supporting text. On 200 labeled questions, unsupported claims fell from 12% to 3%. It fails when retrieval misses the right passage, and the check adds one extra model call."),
 ("RAG vs fine-tune",
  "RAG is more flexible and cost-effective, and it scales better for our use case.",
  "The facts change weekly. Retrieval just re-indexes, while fine-tuning needs a new run each time. RAG adds a 50 to 150 ms hop and fails when the right chunk is outside the top 5. I would fine-tune for format, not for facts."),
 ("10x traffic",
  "We would scale horizontally and leverage the cloud to ensure high availability.",
  "Find the bottleneck first. For an 8B model the KV cache costs 128 KiB per token, so 100 chats at 4,096 tokens need 50 GiB. I would cap context, batch requests and queue bursts. The price is higher p99 latency."),
]

def score(text):
    hits = {k: bool(re.search(p, text, re.I)) for k, p in MARKS.items()}
    fluff = len(re.findall(FLUFF, text, re.I))
    return hits, fluff, len(text.split())

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"{'topic':<18}{'kind':<8}{'num':>4}{'mech':>6}{'fail':>6}{'trade':>6}{'fluff':>7}{'words':>7}")
    for name, weak, strong in PAIRS:
        for kind, text in (("weak", weak), ("strong", strong)):
            h, f, w = score(text)
            print(f"{name:<18}{kind:<8}{int(h['number']):>4}{int(h['mechanism']):>6}{int(h['failure']):>6}{int(h['tradeoff']):>6}{f:>7}{w:>7}")

What to notice

  1. All five weak answers score 0 on number, mechanism, failure and tradeoff. All five strong answers score 4 of 4.
  2. The weak answers carry 2 to 4 fluff words each (streamlined, optimized, robust, leverage) and the strong ones carry none. The fluff is where the missing content should be.
  3. The strong answers are 40 to 54 words against 12 to 18, about 16 to 22 seconds spoken. Depth cost you under 20 extra seconds.
  4. The strong "10x traffic" answer uses the KV cache arithmetic from station 4: 128 KiB per token times 100 chats times 4,096 tokens is 50 GiB. A real number replaced "scale horizontally".
  5. Read the weak answers aloud. They sound fine. That is why this habit takes deliberate practice.
Break it
  1. Stuff it. Run python3 -c "import kata; print(kata.score('Because 7 queue breaks but the price is extra, then cache misses.'))". Predict: does nonsense score high? It scores 4 of 4. The detector sees keywords, not meaning. Lesson: a human, or you on a recording, must check that the parts connect.
  2. Starve it. Run python3 -c "import re,kata; print(kata.score(re.sub(r'\d','#',kata.PAIRS[4][2])))", which blanks every digit in the 10x traffic answer. Predict which marker flips. Only number goes to 0, and the answer drops to 3 of 4 while still sounding technical. Lesson: a number is the part people skip, and the one that makes it checkable.
  3. Surprise it. Run python3 -c "import kata; print(kata.score(kata.PAIRS[3][2].replace('fails','degrades')))", which swaps one word in the RAG answer. Predict the column. Failure drops to 0, though the engineer idea is unchanged. Lesson: the detector knows about 20 words; you know the idea.

Scenario: the interviewer says "How do you optimize tokens?" You have 90 seconds. Build the answer.
  1. Clarify the goal in one clause: cost, latency or fitting a context window. Each has a different lever.
  2. Measure first: log tokens per call by part: system prompt, retrieved context, history, output. Most of the bill sits in one or two parts.
  3. Pick the biggest lever and explain it: a long fixed system prompt means prefix caching (stable content first, dynamic content last). Long history means summarizing or truncating. Large retrieved context means a smaller top k or reranking. Long outputs mean an output cap or a tighter format.
  4. Name the failure mode: caching breaks when anything dynamic sits before the prefix; truncation drops facts the model needed; a smaller top k lowers recall.
  5. Name the price and the number: quality against cost, shown by an eval before and after. Quote the share of tokens saved and the quality delta.
  6. Close with one business sentence. The full playbook is topic G01; this page only shows how to say it.

Traps

Each one sounds right. Say what is wrong with it before you open it.

"Engineer voice means using more technical jargon."
Jargon names things; a mechanism explains them. Five tool names with no explanation is still business voice in a technical costume. The strong answers in the kata use few terms and explain each one.
"Any number makes an answer technical."
The break-it sentence "Because 7 queue breaks but the price is extra, then cache misses" scores 4 of 4 and means nothing. A number without a unit, baseline and mechanism cannot be checked. "40% faster" invites "than what, measured how?"
"Business framing is bad, so leave it out completely."
You aim to be credible as both CTO and CEO. The business sentence belongs, last and short, after the mechanism. What fails is business framing used instead of the mechanism.
"If I don't remember the exact figure, I should give a confident estimate."
Say it is an estimate, or say how you would measure it. A confident invented number collapses at the first follow-up and costs you the credibility of everything else you said.

Question bank

Answer aloud, then open. Grade yourself honestly.

What are the four parts of an engineer-voice claim, and what does each answer?
Mechanism: how does it work, per request or per token. Number: how much, with unit and baseline. Failure mode: how does it break. Tradeoff: what does it cost, on a named axis. A business sentence follows.
Follow-upWhich part do you think founders skip most? Judgement, not fact: usually the failure mode, because a pitch avoids saying where the thing breaks. Say it in your own words and give an example.
Rewrite: "We made our agent more reliable."
Ask what failed first. Example: "Tool calls failed on malformed arguments, so I validate arguments against the tool's schema and retry once with the error text. On a 100-case set, failed runs went from 14 to 4 (illustrative). It costs one extra model call on failures and cannot fix a wrong tool choice."
Follow-upHow do you know 100 cases is enough? It is small. Say what the interval is and what you would add: more cases from real traces.
Why quote p95 and not the average for latency?
The mean hides the tail. If 1 request in 20 takes 3 s while the rest take 300 ms, the mean looks fine and 5% of users wait ten times longer. p95 shows the experience of the slow ones.
Follow-upWhen do you quote p99? When timeouts, retries or SLAs are set near the tail, or when each user session makes many calls, so that most users hit a p99 request.
A candidate says "we cut cost by 40%". Name three follow-up questions the interviewer will ask.
From what baseline, and over what workload? Measured how: per request, per token, per month? What changed to cause it, and what did quality do? Variants: what did you give up, and what happens when it breaks.
What do you do when you did not measure a number?
Say it is an estimate and show the arithmetic, or say what you would measure and how. Never invent. In an interview you can also say "I don't have that figure; here is how I'd get it", which reads as engineer behavior.
An 8B model uses 128 KiB of KV cache per token. How much memory do 100 chats of 4,096 tokens need, and what changes if the model had 32 KV heads instead of 8?
100 x 4,096 x 128 KiB = 51,200 MiB = 50 GiB. With 32 KV heads the per-token cost is 4 times higher: 512 KiB, so 200 GiB. The config is from memory, so verify before quoting.
Follow-upWhat do you do about it? Cap context, quantize the cache, batch carefully, or use a model with fewer KV heads. Each costs quality, speed or capacity.
Rewrite: "We used RAG for accuracy."
"The facts change weekly. Retrieval re-indexes in minutes, where fine-tuning needs a new run. RAG adds a 50 to 150 ms hop (illustrative) and fails when the right chunk is outside the top 5, so I track recall at 5. I would fine-tune for format, not facts."
How long should a first answer run, and what do you do after it?
About 20 seconds for the outcome and the headline mechanism, then pause. Let the interviewer pull. Offer depth in layers: mechanism next, then internals and limits. A full 90 seconds is the ceiling, not the target.
The interviewer says "that sounds like marketing." What do you do?
Take it as a request for mechanism. Drop a level at once: "Fair. Concretely, here is what happens per request." Then give the step you changed, the number, and where it breaks. Do not defend the claim.
Pick one decision from your own work and give it in five parts: claim, mechanism, number, failure, tradeoff. Time yourself.
Self-graded. Pass means: a claim in one clause, a mechanism a stranger could follow, a number that is yours or labeled as an estimate, one real failure case, and a named axis for the price. Under 25 seconds.

Mixed review

Three questions from topics scheduled earlier or alongside this one in week 1. Nobody tells you the topic in an interview. Answers are from memory, so check them against those pages.

D01: What does an LLM do at each step of generating text?
It reads all tokens so far, produces a probability for each possible next token, one token is chosen from that distribution, and it is appended. Then it repeats until a stop token or a length limit.
K01: Why is inference a separate engineering problem from training?
Training is a large, planned job tuned for throughput. Inference runs on every user request, so the targets are latency, cost per token and memory under many concurrent users, with load you do not control.
L01: Why ask clarifying questions before drawing a design?
Requirements set the design: users, request rate, latency target, read and write mix, consistency, failure tolerance and cost. Without them the design cannot be judged, and you may build for the wrong scale.
