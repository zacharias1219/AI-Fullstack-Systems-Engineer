Vectors, dot product, norms, cosine similarity

Every token embedding, retrieval chunk, and attention head speaks in vectors in R^d. How do dot product, cosine similarity, and Euclidean distance actually compare two vectors, when do they disagree on the top result, and why does one normalization line at index time speed up search by an order of magnitude? This page covers vectors in R^d, algebraic and geometric dot product, L1, L2, and L_infinity norms, unit normalization, and metric ranking divergence. Matrix multiplication and broadcasting are A09; retrieval bi-encoder benchmarks are H01.

Query q = [4.0, 0.0] searches three candidate vectors: doc_clean = [0.9, 0.1] (angle 6.3 deg), doc_verbose = [3.2, 2.4] (angle 36.9 deg), and doc_spam = [6.0, 8.0] (angle 53.1 deg). Do raw dot product, raw Euclidean (L2) distance, and cosine similarity agree on the winner?
No. Every metric picks a different winner. Cosine similarity picks doc_clean (0.9939) because it only measures angle. Raw dot product picks doc_spam (24.00 vs 3.60) because its L2 norm of 10.0 overwhelms its 53.1 deg angle. Raw L2 distance picks doc_verbose (distance 2.5298 vs 3.1016 for doc_clean) because its norm of 4.0 matches the query norm of 4.0.
Two vectors u and v are L2-normalized so ||u||_2 = ||v||_2 = 1, and their dot product is u . v = 0.80. Without touching coordinates or a square root, what is their squared Euclidean distance ||u - v||_2^2?
Exactly 0.40. Expand ||u - v||_2^2 = ||u||_2^2 + ||v||_2^2 - 2(u . v). Because both vectors have unit L2 norm, ||u||_2^2 = 1 and ||v||_2^2 = 1, so ||u - v||_2^2 = 2(1 - u . v) = 2(1 - 0.80) = 0.40.
You search 100,000 float32 vectors of width d = 384 with cosine similarity. If you L2-normalize the vectors once at index time and run a plain dot product at query time instead of computing norms on the fly, is the query speedup closer to 1.2x, 3x, or 15x?
About 15x. In the kata benchmark, on-the-fly cosine similarity takes 61.44 ms per query while a pre-normalized dot product takes 4.02 ms per query, a 15.3x speedup with a maximum numerical difference of 5.7e-08.

The core idea

A vector x in R^d is an ordered list of d real numbers that represents a point in d-dimensional space or an arrow from the origin. Every arrow has two separable properties: its direction (the unit ray it points along, where semantic meaning usually lives) and its magnitude or norm (how long the arrow is, which often encodes document length, token frequency, or model confidence). The dot product multiplies both lengths by the cosine of the angle between them: a . b = ||a||_2 ||b||_2 cos(theta).

Because raw dot product mixes direction with length, a noisy vector with a large norm can outscore a clean vector that points almost directly at your query. Cosine similarity strips length out by dividing each vector by its L2 norm, projecting every point onto the surface of the unit sphere. Once all vectors sit on that unit sphere, dot product, cosine similarity, and negative Euclidean distance become mathematically equivalent in rank order.

Mental model. Picture a lighthouse beam shining along the query vector q. Raw dot product measures how far a ship's shadow reaches along the beam multiplied by how tall the mast is, so a giant barge 53 degrees off course casts a bigger score than a small kayak right in the beam. L2 normalization pulls every boat onto a fixed circular buoy ring of radius 1 around the lighthouse; on that ring, largest shadow (dot product), smallest angle (cosine), and shortest straight-line rope (Euclidean distance) always pick the exact same boat.

Memory hook

D-N-S: Dot is length times angle, Norms measure the arrow, Sphere locks the triple. Write a . b = ||a||_2 ||b||_2 cos(theta). On the unit sphere (||u||_2 = ||v||_2 = 1), expand ||u - v||_2^2 = 1 + 1 - 2 cos(theta) = 2(1 - cos(theta)): one formula gives you dot, cosine, and L2 distance at once.

How it works

Vectors in R^d and the two faces of the dot product

An embedding model or transformer layer represents a token or document as a column of d floating-point numbers x = [x_1, x_2, ..., x_d] in R^d (for example d = 384 in small sentence encoders or d = 4,096 in 8B LLMs). Given two vectors a and b in R^d, the algebraic dot product (or inner product) multiplies matching coordinates and adds them up, costing d multiplications and d - 1 additions (2d - 1 FLOPs). The geometric dot product comes from applying the law of cosines to the triangle formed by a, b, and a - b:

Algebraic:  a . b = sum_{i=1..d} a_i * b_i
Law of cosines: ||a - b||_2^2 = ||a||_2^2 + ||b||_2^2 - 2 * ||a||_2 * ||b||_2 * cos(theta)
Expand LHS:     sum (a_i - b_i)^2 = sum a_i^2 + sum b_i^2 - 2 * sum a_i * b_i
Equate terms:   a . b = ||a||_2 * ||b||_2 * cos(theta)

When theta = 0 deg, cos(theta) = 1 and the vectors point in the same direction. When theta = 90 deg, cos(theta) = 0 and the vectors are orthogonal (a . b = 0, sharing no linear component). When theta = 180 deg, cos(theta) = -1 and they point in opposite directions.

Measuring size: L1, L2, and L_infinity norms

A norm ||x|| is a function that assigns a non-negative length to a vector, satisfying positivity, scaling (||c * x|| = |c| * ||x||), and the triangle inequality (||x + y|| <= ||x|| + ||y||). Three norms from the L_p family (||x||_p = (sum |x_i|^p)^(1/p)) show up constantly in AI engineering:

L1 (Manhattan / taxicab):  ||x||_1   = sum_{i=1..d} |x_i|
L2 (Euclidean length):     ||x||_2   = sqrt(sum_{i=1..d} x_i^2) = sqrt(x . x)
L_inf (Max / Chebyshev):   ||x||_inf = max_{i=1..d} |x_i|
Ordering in R^d:           ||x||_inf <= ||x||_2 <= ||x||_1 <= sqrt(d) * ||x||_2

In the kata, doc_verbose = [3.2, 2.4] has ||x||_inf = 3.20, ||x||_2 = 4.00, and ||x||_1 = 5.60, matching 3.20 <= 4.00 <= 5.60 <= sqrt(2) * 4.00 = 5.66. L1 promotes sparsity in regularization because its unit ball has sharp corners on the coordinate axes; L_infinity governs worst-case quantization clipping error (absmax scaling divides by ||x||_inf); L2 is rotation-invariant and defines standard geometric length.

Check: for the vector x = [3, -4], compute ||x||_1, ||x||_2, ||x||_inf, and its L2-normalized unit vector x_hat.
||x||_1 = |3| + |-4| = 7. ||x||_2 = sqrt(3^2 + (-4)^2) = sqrt(9 + 16) = 5. ||x||_inf = max(3, 4) = 4. The unit vector is x_hat = x / ||x||_2 = [3/5, -4/5] = [0.6, -0.8], which satisfies 0.6^2 + (-0.8)^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1.0.

Unit normalization and the unit-sphere equivalence

Dividing any non-zero vector v by its Euclidean norm produces a unit vector v_hat = v / ||v||_2 with ||v_hat||_2 = 1. Cosine similarity between two vectors a and b is defined as the dot product of their L2-normalized unit vectors:

cos(a, b) = (a . b) / (||a||_2 * ||b||_2) = a_hat . b_hat   (range: -1 to +1)
For unit vectors u, v (||u||_2 = ||v||_2 = 1):
||u - v||_2^2 = ||u||_2^2 + ||v||_2^2 - 2(u . v) = 1 + 1 - 2 * cos(theta) = 2 * (1 - cos(theta))

This identity is one of the most useful formulas in applied ML. Because ||u - v||_2^2 = 2 - 2 * cos(theta) is a strictly decreasing linear function of cos(theta), sorting unit-normalized candidates by highest dot product, highest cosine similarity, or smallest Euclidean distance always yields the exact same ranking. In the kata, after L2 normalization, doc_clean has dot = cos = 0.9939 and ||u - v||_2^2 = 2(1 - 0.9939) = 0.0122, winning on all three metrics.

Why pre-normalizing at index time gives a 15x query speedup

Suppose you store N = 100,000 document embeddings of width d = 384 in a matrix X of shape (N, d). If you compute cosine similarity on the fly for a query q, every query must compute the matrix-vector product X @ q (about 2Nd FLOPs), square and sum every row of X to get ||X_i||_2 (another 2Nd FLOPs plus N square roots and a full second pass over 153.6 MB of memory), and divide N elements. If instead you store X_unit = X / ||X||_2 once when indexing and normalize q once per query (only 2d FLOPs), query-time scoring reduces to a single hardware-fused BLAS matrix-vector multiply X_unit @ q_unit. In the kata, that single change cuts latency from 61.44 ms to 4.02 ms per query (15.3x faster).

Check: why does on-the-fly cosine similarity cost 15.3x more time than pre-normalized dot product in NumPy when the raw FLOP count is only about 2x to 3x higher?
X_unit @ q_unit runs inside a single multi-threaded BLAS SGEMV kernel over contiguous memory. Computing np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1) on the fly allocates temporary arrays, reads the 153.6 MB matrix from RAM a second time in a single-threaded reduction, and runs N square roots and divisions. It is memory-bandwidth and allocation bound.

When dot product and cosine rankings diverge

Dot product and cosine similarity produce different rankings whenever candidate vectors have unequal L2 norms. Specifically, candidate B beats candidate A under raw dot product while losing under cosine similarity whenever cos(q, B) < cos(q, A) but ||B||_2 * cos(q, B) > ||A||_2 * cos(q, A), which happens as soon as ||B||_2 / ||A||_2 > cos(q, A) / cos(q, B).

Sometimes that divergence is a bug, and sometimes it is by design. In text retrieval with unnormalized embeddings (or mean-pooled token embeddings without a final norm layer), repeated words or longer chunks inflate ||d||_2, letting repetitive boilerplate hijack top-k search. Conversely, inside transformer self-attention (q_i . k_j / sqrt(d_k)) and the LLM output unembedding layer (h . w_v), raw dot product is used deliberately: a larger vector norm allows the model to express high confidence and sharpen the softmax distribution, whereas cosine similarity caps every score in [-1, 1] unless multiplied by a learned temperature scale.

Check: Candidate A has angle 15 deg (cos = 0.966) and ||A||_2 = 1.0. Candidate B has angle 60 deg (cos = 0.500). At what minimum norm ||B||_2 does B overtake A under raw dot product?
B overtakes A when ||B||_2 * cos(60 deg) > ||A||_2 * cos(15 deg), so ||B||_2 > 0.966 / 0.500 = 1.932. Any norm above 1.94 lets a vector 60 degrees off target beat a vector 15 degrees on target.

Kata: norms, ranking inversions, and the 15x pre-norm speedup

It verifies hand-written L1, L2, and L_infinity norms, dot product, cosine similarity, and squared L2 distance against NumPy, constructs a 3-document ranking scenario where raw dot product, raw L2 distance, and cosine similarity each pick a different winner, proves that L2 normalization collapses all three into the same ranking, and benchmarks 100,000-vector search at d = 384.

"""Vectors, norms, dot product & cosine similarity. Run: python kata.py"""
import argparse, time
import numpy as np

ap = argparse.ArgumentParser()
ap.add_argument("--norm", choices=["l2", "l1", "none"], default="l2")
ap.add_argument("--zero", action="store_true", help="inject a zero vector")
args = ap.parse_args()

def l1_hand(v): return sum(abs(x) for x in v)
def l2_hand(v): return sum(x * x for x in v) ** 0.5
def linf_hand(v): return max(abs(x) for x in v)
def dot_hand(a, b): return sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))
def cos_hand(a, b, eps=0.0):
    return dot_hand(a, b) / (max(l2_hand(a) * l2_hand(b), eps))
def l2sq_hand(a, b): return sum((x - y) ** 2 for x, y in zip(a, b))

q = [4.0, 0.0]  # query vector (||q||_2 = 4.0)
docs = {
    "doc_clean":   [0.9, 0.1],   # concise on-topic note (theta = 6.3 deg, ||d||_2 = 0.91)
    "doc_verbose": [3.2, 2.4],   # 4x longer tangential note (theta = 36.9 deg, ||d||_2 = 4.00)
    "doc_spam":    [6.0, 8.0],   # keyword-stuffed boilerplate (theta = 53.1 deg, ||d||_2 = 10.00)
}
if args.zero:
    docs["doc_empty"] = [0.0, 0.0]

print("1. Hand vs NumPy verification (max abs diff across norms, dot, cos, l2sq):")
q_np = np.array(q)
for name, d in docs.items():
    d_np = np.array(d)
    diffs = [
        abs(l1_hand(d) - np.linalg.norm(d_np, 1)),
        abs(l2_hand(d) - np.linalg.norm(d_np, 2)),
        abs(linf_hand(d) - np.linalg.norm(d_np, np.inf)),
        abs(dot_hand(q, d) - float(q_np @ d_np)),
        abs(l2sq_hand(q, d) - float(np.sum((q_np - d_np) ** 2))),
    ]
    print(f"  {name:<12} L1={l1_hand(d):5.2f}  L2={l2_hand(d):5.2f}  Linf={linf_hand(d):5.2f}  max_err={max(diffs):.1e}")

print("\n2. Raw (unnormalized) rankings (magnitude corrupts both dot and L2):")
print(f"  {'candidate':<12}{'angle':>8}{'L2_norm':>9}{'raw_dot':>9}{'cos_sim':>9}{'raw_L2_dist':>13}")
raw_rows = []
for name, d in docs.items():
    c = cos_hand(q, d)
    deg = np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip(c, -1.0, 1.0)))
    raw_rows.append((name, deg, l2_hand(d), dot_hand(q, d), c, l2sq_hand(q, d) ** 0.5))
    print(f"  {name:<12}{deg:6.1f} deg{l2_hand(d):8.2f}{dot_hand(q, d):9.2f}{c:9.4f}{l2sq_hand(q, d)**0.5:13.4f}")

print("  Rank by raw dot     :", [r[0] for r in sorted(raw_rows, key=lambda r: -r[3])])
print("  Rank by raw -L2 dist:", [r[0] for r in sorted(raw_rows, key=lambda r: r[5])])
print("  Rank by cosine sim  :", [r[0] for r in sorted(raw_rows, key=lambda r: -r[4])])

def normalize(v, mode):
    v = np.asarray(v, dtype=np.float64)
    if mode == "l2": return v / np.linalg.norm(v, 2)
    if mode == "l1": return v / np.linalg.norm(v, 1)
    return v

print(f"\n3. After {args.norm.upper()} normalization (identity ||u-v||_2^2 = 2(1 - u.v) holds on unit sphere):")
qn = normalize(q, args.norm)
norm_rows = []
for name, d in docs.items():
    dn = normalize(d, args.norm)
    dot_v = float(qn @ dn)
    cos_v = float((qn @ dn) / (np.linalg.norm(qn) * np.linalg.norm(dn)))
    l2_sq = float(np.sum((qn - dn) ** 2))
    formula_sq = 2.0 * (1.0 - dot_v)
    norm_rows.append((name, dot_v, cos_v, l2_sq))
    print(f"  {name:<12} dot={dot_v:7.4f}  cos={cos_v:7.4f}  ||u-v||^2={l2_sq:7.4f}  2(1-dot)={formula_sq:7.4f}")

r_dot = [r[0] for r in sorted(norm_rows, key=lambda r: -r[1])]
r_cos = [r[0] for r in sorted(norm_rows, key=lambda r: -r[2])]
r_l2  = [r[0] for r in sorted(norm_rows, key=lambda r: r[3])]
print(f"  Rankings identical? {r_dot == r_cos == r_l2} -> {r_dot}")

print("\n4. Throughput benchmark: N=100,000 vectors, d=384 (float32, 50 runs):")
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
X = rng.standard_normal((100_000, 384), dtype=np.float32)
qv = rng.standard_normal(384, dtype=np.float32)
X_unit = X / np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
q_unit = qv / np.linalg.norm(qv)

t0 = time.perf_counter()
for _ in range(50):
    scores_cos = (X @ qv) / (np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1) * np.linalg.norm(qv) + 1e-12)
ms_cos = (time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000 / 50

t0 = time.perf_counter()
for _ in range(50):
    scores_dot = X_unit @ q_unit
ms_dot = (time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000 / 50
max_diff = float(np.max(np.abs(scores_cos - scores_dot)))
print(f"  On-the-fly cosine : {ms_cos:6.2f} ms/query")
print(f"  Pre-normalized dot: {ms_dot:6.2f} ms/query  ({ms_cos / ms_dot:.1f}x faster, max_diff={max_diff:.1e})")

What to notice

  1. Every hand formula matches NumPy to 0.0e+00, and the norms of every vector obey ||x||_inf <= ||x||_2 <= ||x||_1: for doc_verbose = [3.2, 2.4], 3.20 <= 4.00 <= 5.60.
  2. Before normalization, the three metrics completely disagree on the winner for q = [4.0, 0.0]: raw dot product ranks doc_spam first (24.00 vs 3.60), raw L2 distance ranks doc_verbose first (2.5298 vs 3.1016), and only cosine similarity ranks doc_clean first (0.9939).
  3. doc_verbose beats doc_clean on raw L2 distance even though its angle is 6x worse (36.9 deg vs 6.3 deg) because its length ||B||_2 = 4.00 matches ||q||_2 = 4.00, whereas doc_clean has length 0.91.
  4. After L2 normalization, dot and cos are identical to 4 decimals (0.9939, 0.8000, 0.6000), ||u - v||_2^2 equals 2(1 - dot) (0.0122, 0.4000, 0.8000), and all three rankings become ['doc_clean', 'doc_verbose', 'doc_spam'].
  5. In the 100,000-vector benchmark at d = 384, pre-normalized dot product runs in 4.02 ms against 61.44 ms for on-the-fly cosine (15.3x faster), with a max float32 discrepancy of 5.7e-08.

Scenario: your clinic RAG pipeline switches from a managed embedding API (which returned unit-normalized vectors) to a self-hosted encoder into a FAISS IndexFlatIP (inner product) index. Suddenly, multi-page intake templates outrank exact 2-line discharge notes. How do you diagnose and fix it?
  1. Inspect vector norms first: sample 100 stored vectors and queries, compute np.linalg.norm(v, 2), and verify whether norms vary with chunk token count instead of sitting at 1.0.
  2. Explain the mechanism: IndexFlatIP computes raw dot product ||q||_2 ||d||_2 cos(theta). Without L2 normalization, longer or repetitive intake templates have larger ||d||_2 and hijack the inner product over concise discharge notes with higher cos(theta).
  3. Fix the query path immediately: normalize incoming query vectors and, if raw vectors are in memory, run faiss.normalize_L2(X) before indexing so inner product equals cosine similarity.
  4. Add an ingestion guard: reject zero-norm chunks, normalize every embedding to unit L2 norm at write time, and add a unit test asserting abs(np.linalg.norm(emb) - 1.0) < 1e-5.
  5. Recalibrate score thresholds: verify downstream cutoff filters against the restored [-1, 1] cosine range and re-run the retrieval evaluation set.

1 Outcome 20 sDot product, cosine similarity, and Euclidean distance are the three ways we score vector similarity in embeddings and transformers. Knowing how vector magnitude separates them lets you prevent noisy vectors from hijacking retrieval rankings and speeds up cosine search by over 15x in production.
2 Mechanism 45 sAlgebraically, the dot product sums elementwise products in 2d minus 1 FLOPs; geometrically, it equals the L2 norm of a times the L2 norm of b times cosine theta. Because raw dot product scales with length, a verbose or repetitive document with a huge L2 norm can outscore a concise document at a much smaller angle. Cosine similarity removes length by dividing each vector by its L2 norm, projecting every point onto the unit sphere. On that unit sphere, squared Euclidean distance is 2 times 1 minus cosine theta, so maximizing dot product, maximizing cosine, and minimizing L2 distance produce identical rankings.
3 Internals and limits 25 sIn production, we L2-normalize vectors once at index time so query-time cosine becomes a single BLAS matrix-vector dot product, cutting 100,000-vector search at dimension 384 in my benchmark from 61.4 ms down to 4.0 ms. Only keep raw dot product when magnitude intentionally carries confidence, like transformer attention or unembedding logits, and always clamp norms against zero vectors producing NaN.

Say it like an engineer

Question: how do you choose between cosine similarity, dot product, and Euclidean distance for a vector index?

"Cosine similarity is usually best for text embeddings because it looks at the angle instead of the size of the vector, while dot product is faster to compute."

"You do not have to trade cosine quality for dot-product speed. If you L2-normalize embeddings once at index time and normalize the query vector, unit vectors satisfy ||u - v||_2^2 = 2(1 - u . v), so dot product, cosine similarity, and Euclidean distance give the exact same ranking. In my benchmark on 100,000 384-d float32 vectors, pre-normalized dot product ran in 4.02 ms versus 61.44 ms for on-the-fly cosine, a 15.3x speedup. I only use unnormalized dot product when the encoder was specifically trained for Maximum Inner Product Search where norm encodes document importance."

The strong answer states the algebraic equivalence on the unit sphere, quotes the concrete latency speedup from the toy benchmark (noting it is from your own NumPy run), and names the exact exception when unnormalized dot product is appropriate.

Traps

Each one sounds right. Say what is wrong with it before you open it.

"Euclidean (L2) distance and cosine similarity measure completely different geometries, so an L2-only vector index cannot rank by cosine similarity."
On raw vectors they differ, as the kata showed (raw L2 picked doc_verbose while cosine picked doc_clean). On L2-normalized unit vectors, ||u - v||_2^2 = 2(1 - cos(theta)). Because 2(1 - c) is strictly decreasing in c, an L2-only index fed unit-normalized vectors returns the exact same ranking as cosine similarity.
"Normalizing vectors with L1 norm (so absolute values sum to 1) works just as well as L2 normalization for turning cosine similarity into a dot product."
No. Cosine similarity is defined with L2 norms: (a . b) / (||a||_2 ||b||_2). L1 normalization puts vectors on a flat-sided diamond where diagonal vectors in R^d have L2 norm 1 / sqrt(d) while axis-aligned vectors have L2 norm 1, distorting dot products by up to a factor of d.
"If a cosine similarity score is 0.5, the two vectors are halfway (45 degrees) between identical and orthogonal."
No. Cosine is non-linear in angle: cos(60 deg) = 0.5, whereas cos(45 deg) = 0.7071. At 60 degrees in 2D, the chord distance on the unit circle is already 1.0, the same as the radius.
"You should always L2-normalize query and key vectors inside transformer self-attention so long vectors do not dominate attention weights."
Standard scaled dot-product attention uses q . k / sqrt(d_k) without unit normalization because vector magnitude lets the head vary attention entropy: a large query/key norm sharpens softmax onto one token, while a small norm attends uniformly. If you use cosine attention, you must multiply by a learned temperature scale tau or softmax saturates to a flat distribution.

Explain vectors, dot product, norms, and cosine similarity in 90 seconds.
Use the three-level answer above: outcome (why magnitude vs direction matters for ranking and speed), mechanism (algebraic and geometric dot product, L2 normalization onto the unit sphere, and ||u - v||_2^2 = 2(1 - cos)), and internals/limits (15.3x speedup at index time, attention logits exception, zero-vector NaN guard).
Derive the formula connecting squared Euclidean distance and cosine similarity on unit vectors.
Write ||u - v||_2^2 = (u - v) . (u - v) = ||u||_2^2 + ||v||_2^2 - 2(u . v). When ||u||_2 = 1 and ||v||_2 = 1, u . v = cos(theta), so ||u - v||_2^2 = 1 + 1 - 2 cos(theta) = 2(1 - cos(theta)).
Follow-upIf FAISS IndexFlatL2 returns a squared distance of 0.40 on unit-normalized vectors, what is their cosine similarity? Solve 0.40 = 2(1 - cos), giving cos = 1 - 0.20 = 0.80.
Construct a concrete 2D example where raw dot product and cosine similarity disagree on the winner.
From the kata: let query q = [4, 0]. Let Candidate A = [0.9, 0.1] (angle 6.3 deg, ||A||_2 = 0.91) and Candidate B = [3.2, 2.4] (angle 36.9 deg, ||B||_2 = 4.0). Cosine ranks A first (0.9939 vs 0.8000), but raw dot product ranks B first (12.80 vs 3.60) because ||B||_2 is 4.4x larger.
Follow-upWhich candidate wins under raw Euclidean distance in that same example, and why? Candidate B wins (distance 2.53 vs 3.10 for A) because ||B||_2 = 4.0 matches ||q||_2 = 4.0, whereas A sits near the origin.
State the definitions of L1, L2, and L_infinity norms and their inequality in R^d.
||x||_1 = sum |x_i|, ||x||_2 = sqrt(sum x_i^2), and ||x||_inf = max |x_i|. For any x in R^d, ||x||_inf <= ||x||_2 <= ||x||_1 <= sqrt(d) * ||x||_2.
Follow-upWhen does ||x||_1 equal sqrt(d) * ||x||_2? When all d coordinates have equal magnitude (|x_1| = |x_2| = ... = |x_d|), pointing along the main diagonal.
Why is pre-normalizing vectors at index time 15x faster than computing cosine similarity at query time, even though the FLOP count only drops by ~3x?
Pre-normalized scoring runs a single BLAS matrix-vector multiply X_unit @ q_unit over contiguous memory. On-the-fly cosine must also compute row norms across X on every query, reading the entire N x d matrix from RAM a second time, allocating intermediate arrays, and running N square roots and divisions.
When would you intentionally use raw dot product instead of cosine similarity?
In transformer attention (q . k / sqrt(d_k)), LLM output unembedding (h . w_v), and Maximum Inner Product Search (MIPS) models where vector magnitude is trained to encode confidence, token salience, or document popularity.
Follow-upWhat happens if you replace raw dot product with raw cosine similarity in an LLM output layer without adding a temperature scalar? Logits get clamped to [-1, 1], so the maximum logit gap is 2 and the softmax distribution cannot become sharp (minimum entropy stays high).
How many FLOPs does it take to score one query vector against N = 100,000 pre-normalized vectors of width d = 384?
Each dot product takes 2d - 1 = 767 FLOPs (384 multiplies and 383 adds), so 100,000 dot products cost about 2 * 100,000 * 384 = 76.8 MFLOPs, plus 3 * 384 FLOPs once to normalize the query.
What happens if an empty text chunk produces a zero vector in a cosine similarity pipeline, and how do you prevent it?
||0||_2 = 0, so dividing by the norm produces NaN (0.0 / 0.0). Because IEEE 754 NaN comparisons are false, NaN scores can corrupt argsort or top-k heaps. Prevent it by filtering empty chunks before embedding, clamping the denominator with max(||v||_2, 1e-12), and asserting np.isfinite on ingest.
Why does absmax int8 quantization depend on the L_infinity norm, and how do you spot a vector that will quantize poorly?
Symmetric int8 quantization scales a vector by 127 / ||x||_inf so the largest coordinate hits 127. If one outlier coordinate makes ||x||_inf close to ||x||_2, all other coordinates get crushed near zero.
Follow-upWhat transform fixes high ||x||_inf / ||x||_2 before quantization without changing dot products? Multiplying both query and document vectors by an orthogonal rotation matrix (such as a randomized Hadamard transform), which preserves L2 norms and dot products while spreading energy evenly across coordinates.
If two unit vectors in R^384 are orthogonal, what are their dot product, cosine similarity, and Euclidean distance?
Dot product is 0, cosine similarity is 0, squared Euclidean distance is 2(1 - 0) = 2, and Euclidean distance is sqrt(2) = 1.4142, regardless of the dimension d.

Mixed review

Three questions from earlier topics. Nobody tells you the topic in an interview.

A01: why does a Python list of 100,000 floats lose by orders of magnitude to a NumPy float32 array when computing a dot product?
A Python list stores 8-byte pointers to boxed PyFloat objects scattered across the heap, requiring type checks and pointer chasing on every element. A NumPy float32 array is a contiguous C buffer of 4-byte floats processed by SIMD vector instructions and cache-friendly BLAS loops.
D01: what is the output of an LLM forward pass before sampling, and how does it become a probability distribution over the vocabulary?
The forward pass outputs a vector of raw unnormalized scores (logits) of length V, computed by taking the dot product of the final hidden state with each row of the unembedding matrix, then softmax exponentiates and normalizes those logits to sum to 1.
D02: why does byte-level BPE start from a base vocabulary of 256 tokens, and what is the cost for scripts underrepresented in the training corpus?
Starting from all 256 UTF-8 byte values guarantees that every string can be encoded with zero unknown tokens. However, scripts with few learned merges must fall back toward raw UTF-8 bytes (3 bytes per Indic character), costing up to 4.75x more tokens in the D02 kata.
