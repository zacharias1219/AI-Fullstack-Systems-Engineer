Principled negotiation

How do you turn a zero-sum price standoff into a multi-issue engineering agreement that expands total joint utility by over 30% without conceding below your reservation floor?

Study path

Before you read

Guess first. A wrong guess now makes the right answer stick later.

In our clinic voice-AI contract kata, the provider's single-issue reservation floor is $195k/yr (44.0 utils) and the buyer's ceiling is $230k/yr (46.0 utils). Why does splitting the price down the middle at $210k/yr fail to close?
Because single-issue positional haggling ignores the non-price terms (1-year term, standard 800ms latency, zero data retention). Under those default restrictive terms, a $210k/yr midpoint yields only 41.50 utils for the provider, which falls 2.50 utils below the provider's 44.0-util BATNA reservation floor.
If two parties weight four contract variables asymmetrically (e.g. the provider weights contract term at 0.25 vs the buyer's 0.10, while the buyer weights p99 latency SLA at 0.25 vs the provider's 0.10), how much joint utility can a 3-option MESO trade unlock over the $210k positional midpoint?
In our kata, trading a 3-year term and 90-day de-identified eval logs for a dedicated p99 < 450ms SLA (MESO B at $225k/yr) raises joint utility from 103.75 to 142.40 utils (+37.3%), putting both provider (79.90 utils) and buyer (62.50 utils) well above their BATNA floors.
When you present three Multiple Equivalent Simultaneous Offers (MESOs) to a counterpart, should you rank them from your most preferred to least preferred?
Never. All three packages must yield roughly equal utility to you (e.g. 76.25 to 79.90 provider utils in our kata) and be presented simultaneously with equal enthusiasm. Ranking them leaks your internal utility weights, whereas equal-utility packages force the counterpart to reveal their relative weights by how they react to the tradeoffs.

The core idea

Positional bargaining treats negotiation as a fixed-pie tug-of-war along a single axis (price or base salary). Every dollar one side wins is a dollar the other loses, and each concession damages trust by rewarding stubbornness. The Harvard Program on Negotiation framework (Fisher, Ury, and Patton; formalized quantitatively by Raiffa) replaces positional haggling with principled negotiation across four pillars: separate people from the problem, focus on underlying interests rather than stated positions, invent multi-issue options for mutual gain (logrolling), and insist on objective criteria for claiming value.

For an AI systems engineer negotiating an enterprise deployment contract, a GPU cluster commitment, or a Staff compensation package, principled negotiation is an optimization problem over asymmetric utility vectors. You first compute each side's BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement), translate those BATNAs into reservation thresholds to verify whether a positive ZOPA (Zone of Possible Agreement) exists, and then construct MESOs (Multiple Equivalent Simultaneous Offers) that trade variables low-cost to you but high-value to them for variables low-cost to them but high-value to you.

Memory hook

IBZ-MO (I Buy Zero Mediocre Offers): Interests over positions -> BATNA sets your walk-away floor -> ZOPA bounds the viable deal range -> MESOs logroll asymmetric weights to expand the pie -> Objective criteria settle the split.

How it works

1. Positions vs interests and the diagnostic why

A position is a concrete single-point demand a counterpart states at the table ("We only sign 1-year vendor contracts" or "We require zero data retention"). An interest is the silent constraint, fear, or incentive that produced that position ("Our CFO got burned by shelfware last year, so we fear paying for year two if physician adoption stays below 60%" or "Our CISO forbids raw patient PHI from entering shared weights").

Positions frequently conflict head-on while underlying interests are compatible. When a clinic buyer demands a 1-year term, haggling to 18 months leaves both sides unhappy. Diagnosing the adoption-risk interest lets you propose a 3-year contract with an objective 60% active-clinician adoption gate at month 9: the provider gets multi-year booking credit (weight 0.25) and the buyer eliminates shelfware risk.

2. BATNA, reservation price, and ZOPA geometry

Your BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement) is not a vague wish or your opening aspiration; it is the concrete action plan you will execute if the current deal dies today (for example, deploying those same reserved GPUs to Clinic Group B at $195k/yr net value, or staying at your current employer at $245k total comp). Your Reservation Price (RP) is the exact utility or dollar threshold equivalent to your BATNA, adjusted for switching costs and risk.

ZOPAWidth = RP_buyer - RP_seller
DealViable <=> (RP_buyer >= RP_seller) AND (U_seller(pkg) >= U_seller(BATNA)) AND (U_buyer(pkg) >= U_buyer(BATNA))

When the buyer's reservation ceiling ($230k/yr in our kata) exceeds the seller's reservation floor ($195k/yr), a positive ZOPA of $35k/yr exists. Any agreement inside `[RP_seller, RP_buyer]` yields positive surplus for both parties relative to walking away. If `RP_buyer < RP_seller` on a single issue, no single-issue ZOPA exists: you must either inject new issues to create a multi-issue ZOPA or execute your BATNA cleanly.

Check: What is the difference between your target (aspiration) price and your reservation price, and which one determines whether you walk away?
Your target price is the optimistic upper bound of the counterpart's ZOPA that guides your opening anchor. Your reservation price is the minimum threshold derived strictly from your BATNA; you walk away only when the final package utility drops below your reservation price.

3. Logrolling and the multi-issue Pareto frontier

Most engineering negotiations involve at least four to six dimensions: base fee, committed volume/term, latency or uptime SLA, data usage rights, payment schedule (Net-15 vs Net-90 or upfront annual prepay), and implementation support. Let party $p \in \{S, B\}$ assign normalized weights $w_{p,i}$ across $K$ issues ($\sum_{i=1}^K w_{p,i} = 1$) and score option level $x_i$ as $v_{p,i}(x_i) \in [0, 100]$. Total package utility is:

U_seller(X) = sum_{i=1..K} w_{S,i} * v_{S,i}(x_i)
U_buyer(X)  = sum_{i=1..K} w_{B,i} * v_{B,i}(x_i)
ParetoGain  = (U_seller(X*) + U_buyer(X*)) - (U_seller(X_mid) + U_buyer(X_mid))

Whenever $w_{S,i} \neq w_{B,i}$, logrolling (trading an issue where $w_{B,i} \gg w_{S,i}$ for an issue where $w_{S,j} \gg w_{B,j}$) shifts the deal outward toward the Pareto frontier. In our kata, upgrading latency SLA from 800ms to dedicated 450ms costs the provider only $0.10 \times (90 - 65) = 2.50$ utils, but grants the buyer $0.25 \times (80 - 25) = 13.75$ utils (+5.5x leverage). Conversely, extending the term from 1 year to 3 years costs the buyer only $0.10 \times (85 - 55) = 3.00$ utils while granting the provider $0.25 \times (100 - 15) = 21.25$ utils (+7.1x leverage).

4. MESOs (Multiple Equivalent Simultaneous Offers) and objective criteria

Even when you suspect an asymmetric trade exists, asking "How much do you care about latency versus contract length?" invites strategic misrepresentation: a savvy buyer claims everything is top priority. Presenting three MESO packages simultaneously solves information asymmetry without surrendering value. You construct Packages A, B, and C so that all three sit around 76 to 80 utils for you, but each stresses a different axis (Package A: premium $240k rate for 300ms pinned-GPU latency and 2-yr term; Package B: $225k rate for 450ms dedicated latency, 3-yr term, and 90-day eval logs; Package C: $210k rate for 3-yr term and full de-identified training rights).

Psychologically, MESOs shift the counterpart's frame from binary rejection ("Should I say yes or no?") to comparative selection ("Which of these three works best for our clinic?"). When they respond, "We prefer Package B, except our CISO wants 60-day log retention instead of 90-day," they have mapped their utility gradient for you. You then anchor any remaining value claim on objective criteria: external market benchmarks (e.g., AWS/GCP p99 reserved H100 pricing, standard Radford/Levels.fyi P75 staff engineering comp bands, or third-party SOC2/HIPAA audit costs) and reciprocal standards ("We apply the exact same SLA credit formula to our top five hospital deployments").

Check: Why does presenting three MESOs simultaneously outperform making three sequential offers over three weeks?
Sequential offers look like positional retreats, training the counterpart to keep rejecting until you hit your floor. Simultaneous equivalent offers signal problem-solving flexibility while proving your total utility threshold is firm, and the counterpart's comparison reveals their hidden issue weights in one turn.

5. W-domain engineering toolkit: phrases, role-play script, and rubric

In high-stakes negotiations (enterprise AI deployments, cloud commit contracts, or Staff+ compensation), the exact words you speak prevent defensive escalation while locking in principled structure:

  • Pivoting from position to interest: "Help me understand the operational constraint behind the 1-year term limit so we design around the actual risk rather than a calendar date."
  • Testing objective criteria: "Walk me through the benchmark or TCO calculation you used to arrive at $180k/yr; if that methodology holds across dedicated p99 < 450ms GPU reservations, we will align with it."
  • Introducing MESOs: "Based on our architecture review, here are three complete configurations that all work for our infrastructure economics. Tell me which one comes closest to your clinical and compliance priorities, and we can fine-tune from there."
  • Defending against a unilateral concession demand: "To lower the annual fee from $225k to $210k while keeping dedicated 450ms latency, we need to rebalance the package on either the 3-year commit or de-identified training logs. Which lever is easier for your team?"

"""
W02 Kata: Principled Negotiation Prep-Sheet & MESO Package Scorer
Scenario: Enterprise Voice-AI Clinic Deployment Contract (Provider vs Health System Buyer)
"""

ISSUES = {
    # issue: (provider_weight, buyer_weight, levels_dict)
    # levels_dict maps option_label -> (provider_score_0_to_100, buyer_score_0_to_100, dollar_impact_k)
    "annual_rate_k": (0.45, 0.50, {
        "$180k/yr": (20, 95, 180),
        "$210k/yr": (55, 65, 210),
        "$225k/yr": (72, 50, 225),
        "$240k/yr": (90, 30, 240),
        "$255k/yr": (100, 10, 255),
    }),
    "contract_term": (0.25, 0.10, {
        "1-year": (15, 85, 0),
        "2-year": (65, 70, 15),
        "3-year": (100, 55, 35),
    }),
    "latency_sla": (0.10, 0.25, {
        "p99 < 800ms (standard)": (90, 25, 0),
        "p99 < 450ms (dedicated)": (65, 80, -8),
        "p99 < 300ms (pinned GPU)": (35, 100, -18),
    }),
    "data_retention": (0.20, 0.15, {
        "Zero-retention (no fine-tune)": (20, 100, -25),
        "De-identified eval logs (90d)": (80, 80, 20),
        "Full de-identified training": (100, 35, 35),
    }),
}

PROVIDER_BATNA_UTILITY = 44.0  # Reservation floor ($195k equivalent net value from next-best clinic deal)
BUYER_BATNA_UTILITY = 46.0     # Reservation floor (building in-house Whisper + LLM wrapper at $230k/yr TCO)

PACKAGES = {
    "Positional haggling ($210k midpoint, 1-yr, 800ms, Zero-ret)": {
        "annual_rate_k": "$210k/yr",
        "contract_term": "1-year",
        "latency_sla": "p99 < 800ms (standard)",
        "data_retention": "Zero-retention (no fine-tune)",
    },
    "MESO A (Speed-first: $240k, 2-yr, 300ms pinned, 90d eval logs)": {
        "annual_rate_k": "$240k/yr",
        "contract_term": "2-year",
        "latency_sla": "p99 < 300ms (pinned GPU)",
        "data_retention": "De-identified eval logs (90d)",
    },
    "MESO B (Balanced-term: $225k, 3-yr, 450ms dedicated, 90d eval logs)": {
        "annual_rate_k": "$225k/yr",
        "contract_term": "3-year",
        "latency_sla": "p99 < 450ms (dedicated)",
        "data_retention": "De-identified eval logs (90d)",
    },
    "MESO C (Data-partner: $210k, 3-yr, 450ms dedicated, Full de-id train)": {
        "annual_rate_k": "$210k/yr",
        "contract_term": "3-year",
        "latency_sla": "p99 < 450ms (dedicated)",
        "data_retention": "Full de-identified training",
    },
}

def score_package(pkg):
    p_util, b_util = 0.0, 0.0
    for issue, choice in pkg.items():
        pw, bw, levels = ISSUES[issue]
        ps, bs, _ = levels[choice]
        p_util += pw * ps
        b_util += bw * bs
    return round(p_util, 2), round(b_util, 2), round(p_util + b_util, 2)

print("=== W02 PRINCIPLED NEGOTIATION: BATNA, ZOPA & MESO SCORER ===")
print(f"Provider BATNA floor : {PROVIDER_BATNA_UTILITY:.1f} utils | Single-issue reservation price: $195k/yr")
print(f"Buyer BATNA floor    : {BUYER_BATNA_UTILITY:.1f} utils | Single-issue reservation ceiling: $230k/yr")
print(f"Single-issue ZOPA    : $195k/yr to $230k/yr (width = $35k/yr)\n")

base_p, base_b, base_j = score_package(PACKAGES["Positional haggling ($210k midpoint, 1-yr, 800ms, Zero-ret)"])
print(f"{'Package Name':<68} | {'Prov':>5} | {'Buyer':>5} | {'Joint':>6} | {'ZOPA?'}")
print("-" * 100)
for name, pkg in PACKAGES.items():
    pu, bu, ju = score_package(pkg)
    viable = "YES" if (pu >= PROVIDER_BATNA_UTILITY and bu >= BUYER_BATNA_UTILITY) else "NO (below BATNA)"
    gain_pct = ((ju - base_j) / base_j) * 100.0
    print(f"{name:<68} | {pu:5.2f} | {bu:5.2f} | {ju:6.2f} | {viable} ({gain_pct:+.1f}%)")

Traps

Each one sounds right. Say what is wrong with it before you open it.

"Principled negotiation means being collaborative and splitting the difference down the middle so both sides compromise equally."
Wrong. Splitting the difference on a single axis is positional bargaining, and it leaves massive value on the table or violates BATNA. In our kata, the $210k price midpoint yields 41.50 provider utils (below the 44.0 BATNA floor) and 103.75 joint utils, whereas multi-issue logrolling (MESO B at $225k) yields 79.90 provider utils, 62.50 buyer utils, and 142.40 joint utils (+37.3%).
"Your BATNA is your ideal target outcome - the highest price or compensation package you hope to get if the negotiation goes well."
Wrong. That is your aspiration (target) price. Your BATNA is your Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement - the concrete fallback action you will execute if this deal fails today. Your BATNA determines your reservation price (walk-away floor), not your target ceiling.
"To avoid overwhelming a counterpart, present your multi-issue packages one at a time, starting with your favorite option."
Wrong. Sequential offers are interpreted as positional concessions and fail to elicit comparative preferences. You must present 3 packages simultaneously (MESOs) that all carry roughly equal utility to you (e.g., 76.25 to 79.90 utils) without revealing any preference ordering.
"If the buyer's maximum budget ($200k) is lower than the seller's minimum standalone price ($215k), no ZOPA exists and you should immediately walk away."
Wrong. No single-issue price ZOPA exists, but adding non-price variables with asymmetric weights (such as annual upfront prepay, a 3-year commit, de-identified eval data rights, or off-peak batch SLA) can lower the seller's effective delivery cost or raise total package utility above the 44.0-util BATNA floor, creating a multi-issue ZOPA.

What are the four pillars of Harvard principled negotiation, and what engineering artifact maps to each pillar?
The four pillars are: (1) Separate people from the problem (use W01 tactical empathy and blame-free architecture reviews); (2) Focus on interests, not positions (diagnose operational constraints like adoption risk or CISO compliance behind rigid demands); (3) Invent options for mutual gain (build a multi-issue weight matrix and 3 iso-utility MESO packages); (4) Insist on objective criteria (anchor pricing on GPU TCO, market comp percentiles, and reciprocal SLA formulas).
Follow-upWhich pillar addresses value creation vs value claiming? Interests and multi-issue options (MESOs) create value (expanding joint utility to the Pareto frontier); BATNA and objective criteria claim value and defend your share inside the ZOPA.
How do you mathematically verify whether a multi-issue package sits inside the Zone of Possible Agreement (ZOPA)?
Compute the weighted utility sum for each party: $U_S(X) = \sum w_{S,i} v_{S,i}(x_i)$ and $U_B(X) = \sum w_{B,i} v_{B,i}(x_i)$. The package sits inside the ZOPA if and only if both parties strictly meet or beat their BATNA reservation thresholds simultaneously: $U_S(X) \ge U_S(\text{BATNA})$ and $U_B(X) \ge U_B(\text{BATNA})$ (in our kata, $\ge 44.0$ and $\ge 46.0$ utils).
Follow-upWhat happens if $U_S(X) + U_B(X)$ increases by 25%, but $U_S(X) < U_S(\text{BATNA})$? The deal is still non-viable for the seller; high joint utility never overrides an individual BATNA violation.
Why does logrolling create positive joint surplus, and what mathematical condition is required on the issue weight vectors?
Logrolling creates surplus by trading concessions on issues you weight lightly for gains on issues you weight heavily. Mathematically, the parties' weight vectors must be non-identical ($w_{S,i} \neq w_{B,i}$ for at least two issues $i, j$): party S concedes on issue $i$ where $w_{B,i} \gg w_{S,i}$ in exchange for party B conceding on issue $j$ where $w_{S,j} \gg w_{B,j}$.
Follow-upIn our kata, what is the exact utility exchange when trading latency SLA (800ms to 450ms) for contract term (1-yr to 3-yr)? Upgrading SLA costs the provider 2.50 utils and gives the buyer +13.75 utils; extending term costs the buyer 3.00 utils and gives the provider +21.25 utils, netting +18.75 provider utils and +10.75 buyer utils (+29.50 joint utils before price adjustment).
Walk through how you construct three Multiple Equivalent Simultaneous Offers (MESOs) before an enterprise contract call.
First, list 4 to 5 negotiable levers (rate, term, latency SLA, data retention, payment schedule) and score each level from 0 to 100 for yourself and estimated for the counterpart. Second, set a target utility band well above your BATNA (e.g., 76 to 80 provider utils vs 44.0 BATNA). Third, assemble three distinct combinations of levers that all score inside that 76-80 util band for you, where Package A optimizes the counterpart's speed/SLA, Package B balances rate and term, and Package C optimizes lowest upfront price via non-cash value (3-yr term + training data).
A procurement buyer looks at your three MESO packages and says: "Give us the $210k price from Package C with the 300ms pinned-GPU latency from Package A and a 1-year term." How do you respond?
Never accept unbundled cherry-picking. Explain the architectural coupling using objective criteria and re-link the levers conditionally: "Pinned 300ms latency requires dedicated GPU reservations that cost $42k/yr in base infrastructure. In Package C, the $210k rate is only economically viable because the 3-year commit and training telemetry offset that compute cost. If we lock in p99 < 300ms on a shorter term, we are back in Package A's $240k structure - or we can keep $210k with the dedicated 450ms SLA and 3-year commit."
Follow-upWhat score does that cherry-picked package ($210k, 1-yr, 300ms, zero-retention) produce in the kata? It yields only 35.75 provider utils ($24.75 + 3.75 + 3.25 + 4.00$), which is 8.25 utils below the provider's 44.0 BATNA floor.
How do you improve your negotiation outcome before you ever speak to the counterpart?
By actively strengthening your own BATNA and researching the counterpart's BATNA switching costs. Since your reservation floor equals your BATNA utility, cultivating a second qualified clinic deployment lead or a competing job offer raises your walk-away floor (e.g., from 44.0 to 58.0 utils), narrows the ZOPA upward in your favor, and gives you genuine calm to decline sub-par packages.
When should you make the first numerical offer (anchor) vs let the counterpart anchor first?
Anchor first when you have strong information about the counterpart's ZOPA and verifiable objective criteria (e.g., TCO benchmarks or market comp data) to justify an ambitious anchor near the top of their ZOPA. Let the counterpart anchor first when information asymmetry is high and you risk anchoring below what they were already prepared to pay; if they anchor first with an extreme lowball, immediately re-anchor using objective criteria rather than splitting the difference.
Follow-upIf a counterpart anchors at $180k/yr (below your $195k reservation floor), why is counter-offering so that $180k and your counter average to $210k dangerous? It validates their sub-ZOPA anchor as a legitimate boundary. Instead, ask them to explain the objective cost model behind $180k, state clearly that $180k does not cover dedicated inference infrastructure, and pivot to your three benchmark-backed MESOs.
How do W01 (Tactical empathy) and W02 (Principled negotiation) work together in a tense customer or compensation negotiation?
W01 tactical empathy (labeling emotions, calibrated "How/What" questions, accusation audits) regulates the counterpart's amygdala and uncovers their unstated constraints without triggering defensiveness (Pillar 1 and Pillar 2 of Harvard negotiation). Once those underlying interests and relative priorities are surfaced, W02 principled negotiation quantifies the BATNA/ZOPA bounds, structures the 3-package MESO logroll, and anchors the final split on objective criteria (Pillars 3 and 4).
What constitutes a valid "objective criterion" in an AI engineering negotiation, and how do you test whether a counterpart's criterion is legitimate?
A valid objective criterion is an external standard independent of either party's willpower: published cloud GPU hourly pricing ($2.10/hr reserved H100), empirical p99 latency load-test curves, peer health-system contract rates, or Levels.fyi / Radford compensation percentiles. You test legitimacy with the reciprocity test: if the counterpart cites a standard to demand a discount, ask whether they apply that exact same formula reciprocally when scope or token volume exceeds forecast.

Mixed review

Three questions from earlier topics. Nobody tells you the topic in an interview.

W01: What is the difference between a tactical empathy label and a calibrated question, and in what order do you deploy them when a stakeholder resists a design?
A label ("It sounds like you are concerned that a 3-year commit locks you into unproven latency") names the counterpart's emotion or constraint without asking a question, disarming defensiveness. A calibrated question ("How would we structure an adoption checkpoint at month 9 so your team never pays for unused seats?") starts with How or What to invite collaborative problem-solving. You deploy the label first (followed by a 4-second silent pause) to earn a "That's right" before asking the calibrated question.
G01: Why does LLM prompt caching only reduce prefill cost and TTFT when the shared context sits at the exact prefix of the prompt?
Because causal self-attention computes each token's Key and Value tensors as a function of all preceding tokens ($t_0 \dots t_i$). Changing even one token at position $k$ invalidates every KV state from position $k$ onward, so KV cache reuse must terminate at the first divergent token.
K06: Write the per-token KV cache memory formula for a decoder-only Transformer and state how GQA reduces it.
$\text{Bytes/token} = 2 \times n_{\text{layers}} \times n_{\text{kv\_heads}} \times d_{\text{head}} \times \text{bytes\_per\_elem}$ (the factor of 2 accounts for Key and Value). Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) shares each KV head across $G = n_{\text{q\_heads}} / n_{\text{kv\_heads}}$ query heads, shrinking KV cache memory and decode bandwidth by a factor of $G$ (typically 4x to 8x).

Short notes

Quick-memorization cheat sheet covering every subtopic on this page and the key facts to remember cold.

All subtopics on this page

  • Interests vs positions: A position is a single-point demand ("1-year term only"); an interest is the underlying risk or constraint ("fear of paying for year-2 shelfware if clinician adoption < 60%"). Diagnose with "Help me understand what drives..." before counter-offering.
  • BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement): Your concrete executable fallback plan if the deal dies today; translates directly into your Reservation Price (RP) walk-away threshold (44.0 provider utils / $195k; 46.0 buyer utils / $230k).
  • ZOPA (Zone of Possible Agreement): The region where both parties beat their BATNA reservation thresholds simultaneously ($[\text{RP}_{\text{seller}}, \text{RP}_{\text{buyer}}] = [\$195\text{k}, \$230\text{k}]$, width $35k/yr single-issue).
  • Logrolling and Pareto expansion: Trading concessions on low-weight issues for gains on high-weight issues when $w_{S,i} \neq w_{B,i}$ (trading dedicated 450ms SLA for 3-year term and 90-day eval logs yields +37.3% joint utility).
  • MESOs (Multiple Equivalent Simultaneous Offers): Presenting 3 complete coupled packages of equal utility to you (76.25 to 79.90 utils) at the same time so the counterpart's comparison reveals their hidden issue weights.
  • Objective criteria: Anchoring value claims on verifiable external standards (reserved H100 hourly TCO, empirical load-test curves, Levels.fyi P75 comp bands) tested for reciprocity.
Important things to remember
  • Memory hook (IBZ-MO): Interests -> BATNA -> ZOPA -> MESOs -> Objective criteria.
  • Why midpoint splitting fails (kata proof): Splitting price at $210k/yr under default 1-yr / 800ms / zero-retention terms yields 41.50 provider utils (below the 44.0 BATNA floor) and 103.75 joint utils.
  • MESO Pareto gain (kata proof): MESO B ($225k, 3-yr, 450ms dedicated, 90d eval logs) scores 79.90 provider utils, 62.50 buyer utils, and 142.40 joint utils (+37.3% over positional midpoint).

  • Conditional trade syntax: "If you need X [price/SLA concession], then we need to pair it with Y [term/data/prepay lever] so the infrastructure economics stay above our floor."
